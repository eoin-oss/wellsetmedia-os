## Core Concept

Branding means deliberately pairing two or more things together. Consumers constantly pair you with things you say, do, appear next to, and look like. The question is whether you control those pairings or let them happen by accident.

**Good branding:** Pairing your business with something a majority of your ideal audience likes.
**Bad branding:** Pairing your business with something a majority of your audience dislikes.
**No branding:** Exposure results in them behaving as normal. You are practically invisible.

**Origin insight:** [CREATOR_NAME] ran a supplement company with the strongest legal blends, 40% affiliate commissions (vs. competitor's 10-20%), and better products. But a close friend would not switch from a worse product with lower payouts. Why? The other company was cool. [CREATOR_NAME] had put his entire budget into commissions and product, but none into building a brand people would think was cool. Even a close friend, who would immediately make another $500,000+ per year by selling his products, would not switch. That showed what was missing.

**Branding is how [CREATOR_NAME] built:** A 10.1 million person audience across platforms in 4 years. Sold over a million copies of two books. Earned deals worth hundreds of millions of dollars through Acquisition.com.

## Learning and Behavior Change

**Learning means: same condition, new behavior.** If you tell someone what to do, practice it, and they do the new thing when the condition occurs, they learned. If they do the old thing, they did not learn.

Most branding definitions describe brand but do not give concrete actions to create one. After reading them, you still do not know what to do. If you do not know what to do, nothing changes. Fundamentally, if you do not change your behavior, nothing changes as a result.

**Litmus test for this content:** If by the end, you change your behavior, you learned. If you do not, it was a waste of time.

Common definitions that sound nice but do not tell you what to do:
- "A brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organization"
- "A brand is the set of expectations, memories, stories and relationships that account for a consumer's decision to choose one product over another"
- "A brand is emotional shorthand for accumulated or assumed information"
- "Branding is the process of creating and disseminating the brand name, its qualities and its personality"
- "The promotion of a particular product or company by means of advertising and distinctive design"

These describe the outcome. They do not describe the actions. The playbook focuses on what you actually do.

## Branding vs. Advertising

Advertising is letting people know about your stuff. Branding is what you let them know in the advertisement.

Advertising lets people know the cow belongs to someone. Branding lets them know the cow belongs to you. You do not want them to buy products like yours, you want them to buy your products.

You can have great advertising and poor branding, or great branding and poor advertising, or any combination. They are separate variables.

**The cattle herder origin:** The first entrepreneurs who used branding to make money were cattle herders. They would literally brand livestock with a hot metal symbol. Those symbols had an effect. They changed people's behavior.

- **No brand cow:** You see a cow with nothing on it. You might leave it alone. That might be it.
- **Branded cow you recognize:** "Hey, that's Bill's cow."
- **Good branding (you like Bill):** You might grab the cow and return it to Bill.
- **Bad branding (you hate Bill):** The cow may stay lost forever and become lunch for your family.
- **Branded cow, unknown brand:** You know it belongs to someone, so you treat it the way you treat branded animals in general. Different from a no-logo cow.

Either way, the brand affected behavior.

## Measuring Brand

| Variable | Belongs To | What It Measures |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Reach** | Advertising | How many people know about your stuff |
| **Influence** | Branding | The chance it changes behavior when they see it |
| **Direction** | Branding | How their behavior changes, towards or away |

**Key insight:** Something that creates a "towards" reaction in one audience creates an "away" reaction in another. Direction depends on the target audience.

**Example using eight brand positions (using [CREATOR_NAME] as the viewer):**
1. High reach, Low influence, Towards: Taylor Swift. In general good, probably will not buy much of her stuff.
2. High reach, High influence, Towards: Elon Musk. Owns two Teslas, uses Starlink, supports whatever he does.
3. High reach, High influence, Away: Terrorists. Strongly opposed.
4. High reach, Low influence, Away: Hollywood actors. Finds them whiny and entitled. Less likely to support generically.
5. Low reach, High influence, Towards: Close friends. Do not have big reach, but stronger influence.
6. Low reach, Low influence, Away: Stranger in the street. Not much reach, not much influence, generally avoids.
7. Low reach, High influence, Away: Copycats and scammers. Most people have not heard of them, but strong influence away.
8. Low reach, Low influence, Towards: Acquaintances. Not many people know them, but generally inclined to support.

All these people have brands. For business owners, we measure success with money. Good branding means your audience likes and buys. Bad branding means the people in your ideal audience hate it and do not buy.

## The Polarization Myth

Many people think strong brands must be polarizing, with lots of lovers and haters. This is not always true.

Donald Trump is a polar brand. People recognize him instantly (big reach) and react strongly (influence), positively or negatively (direction). But you do not have to be polar to build a strong brand.

Taylor Swift, Mother Teresa, and Apple are examples of strong, positive brands. Widely recognized and liked. You do not need controversy to build a strong brand. You can be widely positive. Make promises that serve a large audience, and keep them.

When you sell a product, polarization occurs whether you want it to or not. Selling polarizes people. Deliberately trying to "polarize" interferes with doing business. Just focus on getting people to buy your stuff. If you do that, you will probably sell more of it.

## Publicity Rule

"Any publicity is better than no publicity" is a distortion of two different measurements.

The saying should be: "Any publicity is better than no publicity, but good publicity is better than bad."

Good beats bad. Bad beats none. Lots of good beats everything.

If everyone in the world found out something "bad" about you, you would still be likely to make more sales than if no one knows you exist. Reason: almost nothing is viewed as "bad" to 100% of people. But if everyone in the world found out something good about you, you would certainly be more likely to make money than the reverse.

## Case Study: Kendall Jenner x Pepsi

Pepsi ran an ad featuring Kendall Jenner handing a police officer a Pepsi during a protest. They received immediate backlash and took the ad down within six days.

**Analysis:** Good advertising (lots of people found out) but bad branding (a lot of people hated it). With Pepsi's audience of everyone, they made a bad pairing. They paired their brand with something the majority of people do not find refreshing and joyful: protests.

This actually had very little to do with Jenner, and everything to do with protests. She could have likely done a different ad with Pepsi and it would have been a success. More people saw it because it included Jenner. But the pairing itself was the problem. It netted a loss for the business.

**Counter-question:** Is there a product where the Kendall Jenner + protest pairing could have netted the business money? Yes. It just is not Pepsi.

## Translating Brand Into Sales (Step by Step Process)

1. Start with a brand that means nothing. You have a logo, like the cow the other person did not recognize. People know it is a brand, they just do not know what it means.
2. Pair it with stuff your customers like (people, experiences, outcomes they deem desirable).
3. Your brand starts to mean "thing the customer likes" to them.
4. Buy a generic product.
5. Add your brand to the generic product, transforming it into a premium branded product.
6. Customers want to associate with that positively associated branded product.
7. They exchange their money for your premium product.
8. They get the product and association.
9. You get higher profits, from higher prices, because of those associations.

**The hat example:** A strong brand turns a $10 hat into a premium product. Customers want to pair themselves with the product so they can associate with the outcome the brand delivers. They go from "I want to be an ambitious entrepreneur/investor" to exchanging money and saying "Now I am an ambitious entrepreneur" or "I feel like an ambitious entrepreneur." That is how this works.

**What to pair with:** People your ideal customer admires (e.g., Elon Musk, Warren Buffett for an entrepreneurial audience). Things they consume for value (books, podcasts, tools). Outcomes they deem desirable (wealth, health, status). The pairing transfers some of the influence and reach of what you pair with into your product itself. Then you pair the product with your audience using an offer.

**The premium twist:** A strong brand turns commoditized products into premium products. And premium products get customers to want to pair themselves with the product, which they do by giving you their money.

**Bottom line:** As long as the increased profit from premium pricing covers the cost to make the pairings, you branded yourself well.

## Warren Buffett on Pricing Power

"The single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power. If you've got the power to raise prices without losing business to a competitor, you've got a very good business. And if you have to have a prayer session before raising the price by 10%, then you've got a terrible business."

Branding is how you get pricing power. It is the thing that lets you charge more without losing customers. Without a brand, you compete on price. With a brand, you compete on association and trust.

## How to Build a Brand (4 Steps)

### Step 1: Figure Out Who Your Ideal Customer Is

Four criteria for selecting your target audience:

| Criterion | Why It Matters | Selection Logic |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Growing** | A growing market means you grow even if you stay the same size | If choosing between shrinking, maintaining, and growing, go after growing |
| **Have enough money** | They can afford premium pricing | If you want to charge a lot, they need to have the money |
| **Easy to target** | You can put ads in front of them with little effort | If customers exist but you cannot find them, does it matter? |
| **In pain** | They desperately want what you sell | Competition proves people buy it. Now you just need to make it better. |

**Worked example (law firm for assisted living):**
- Growing: Baby boomers. This population is growing. Check.
- Money: Lots of businesses already serve this population. Check.
- Easy to find: Super easy-to-follow ads targeting older people. Check.
- Pain: Do aging populations have legal issues to contend with? Do they ever. And the businesses bear the burden of those issues. Check.

Assisted living centers meet all qualifications. That is the "who."

**Selection logic:** If you could choose between hard and easy, choose easy. If competition exists, at least you know people are buying. Now you just need to make it better. And most business owners are not tough to beat.

**Brand pivot warning:** [CREATOR_NAME] started making content that got wider and wider. He stopped attracting business owners and started attracting wantrepreneurs. He needed to pivot his associations to make sure he was maximizing with the stuff his ideal customers liked.

### Step 2: Figure Out What They Like

List the things your ideal customer cares about: outcomes they want, problems they struggle with, people they admire, values they hold. These become your pairing targets.

**Worked example (assisted living centers):**
1. Making more money
2. Getting more customers
3. Making more profits
4. Building a sellable business
5. Help recruiting talent so they do not have to work as much
6. Retaining talent (finding quality nurses is expensive with high demand)
7. Fewer lawsuits
8. Paying fewer taxes

### Step 3: Associate Your Brand With Those Things

Three actions:
1. Make content geared towards this audience (videos, blogs, articles, booklets, PDFs)
2. Distribute it
3. Iterate based on audience response

You are building associations between your brand and the things your ideal customer already values. When your content makes their life better, they think of you.

**Content examples (for assisted living law firm):**
- "Here are things we see across our firm for assisted living facilities that helped lower costs and improve quality of life for residents."
- "They can structure their accounting to depreciate their investments this way to save more money."
- "Here is how people set up floor plans. This makes it more efficient so they can house more residents per square foot and demand less of overworked staff."

Give this stuff away. Associate yourself with the things they want and need. When people think "this stuff really makes my life better," they think of your business.

**Think from the customer's perspective:** As you build and reinforce the brand, you have to think about it from the perspective of the customer. They might agree with your stuff at first, and even try it out. But becoming lifelong customers is another hill to climb. You want to make that climb as smooth as possible.

**Content formats to create:** Videos, blogs, articles, booklets, PDFs. Start giving this stuff away. Start associating yourself with the things they want and the things they need. The more formats you create, the more surfaces for pairing.

### Step 4: Optimize Associations for Maximum Profit

Five directions to expand your market:

| Direction | Definition | Example (Law Firm for Assisted Living) |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Up market** | Larger customers | Multi-location assisted living owners |
| **Down market** | Smaller customers | At-home care and mobile providers |
| **Adjacent** | Similar but different market | Rehab facilities |
| **Broader** | The entire category | All senior living centers (hospice, end of life, retirement) |
| **Narrower** | A slice of your existing market | Assisted living with 100-500 residents |

When you move in any direction, you change what you associate with. Your core audience changes, so your brand should change deliberately.

## The Bouquet Metaphor

Think of branding like assembling a bouquet of flowers. Individual products, values, experiences, and people are not a brand on their own, just as individual flowers are not a bouquet. Tying them together forms the brand.

What we choose to include, from where, and how much, influences the brand we ultimately create.

- **Narrowing:** Go from many colors of roses to only red roses. Talk only about specific things. Example: only assisted living in a particular state, or only a particular business model, or over a certain size.
- **Broadening:** Bring all sorts of flowers into the bouquet. Talk about the wider category. Example: from assisted living to all types of retirement homes, end of life, hospice, then real estate communities in general, then investments in general.
- **Adjacent pivot:** Slowly switch from red roses to yellow roses. Still roses, different type. Example: only talk about things specific to end of life facilities.
- **Up market:** Only talk about problems multi-location owners benefit from.
- **Down market:** Talk about more stuff that smaller providers, mobile care, and at-home care people find value in.
- **Mistake recovery:** One ugly flower messes up the whole bouquet. You cannot remove it (it already happened). Overwhelm customers with more of the stuff they like until the bad pairing shrinks into irrelevance. Do not try to eliminate the mistake. It happened. Just overwhelm it with way more of the stuff the majority of your people actually like.

**Real-world mistake recovery:** Kanye West has said things people do not like, but he comes out with products people love. Over successive pairings, the good stuff minimizes the bad stuff, and people still buy.

## Levels of Authority (The Branding Cycle)

The strongest associations form (for better and for worse) when they involve those we know and trust. We can speed this up by using other sources they know and trust.

| Level | Source | Influence |
|-------|--------|-----------|
| **What you say** | Content, ads, outreach | Least influential |
| **What others say** | Affiliates, other customers | Much more influential |
| **What they experience** | Their own use of the product | Most influential, by far |

**Good branding cycle:** Advertise successfully, get the first sale, deliver on promises, strong positive association forms. Make them strong enough and they buy again, telling others to do the same.

**Bad branding cycle:** Advertise successfully, get the first sale, fail to deliver, strong negative association forms. They never buy again and discourage others from buying. This becomes a vicious cycle with each customer chipping away at your reputation.

**Warren Buffett quote:** "Your premium brand had better be delivering something special, or it's not going to get the business." [CREATOR_NAME] adds: it will not keep the business. You might get the first purchase, but not repeat ones. If charging premium prices, make your product exceptional. Your reputation can only get you so far. At some point you have to deliver.

**Brand goes both ways:** Your brand influences the likelihood someone buys the product. Then, the product influences how they see the brand in the future. It also affects everything around it. If someone refers your product and it sucks, they will hate the person for making the recommendation. If it is good, they will be more likely to take recommendations again.

Example: If a porn star recommends an accounting firm, you may assume it is an accounting firm for porn stars. Because of this two-sided relationship, you need to decide what values, people, and experiences you want to use to connect the audience to your product, and equally importantly, what things you want to avoid, remove, or ignore from the stuff they hate.

**The referral chain:** If someone refers your product to a customer, the customer will see that person differently based on how good or bad your product is. If it sucks, they will hate the referrer for making the recommendation. If it is good, they will be more likely to take recommendations from that person again and think more fondly of them. Your product quality ripples outward through every person connected to it.

**How promises work:** The advertisement lets people know about it. The branding makes a good association. They make the purchase. After that, the product does most of the branding. Because if you buy an amazing hat and there is a hole in it when it comes, and it is the first time you ever bought from that company, you will think the whole thing is a sham.

**Bad products create a vicious cycle:** Not only does acquiring new customers get harder, but you do not have your first set of customers buying again and again. This makes it hard to do business. Good products create a virtuous cycle. Each satisfied customer makes the next one easier and cheaper to acquire.

**Key rule:** Your advertising has stronger influence on your brand in the short term. Your product has stronger influence on your brand in the long term. Main reason: people will have far more impactful experiences with the product than your advertisements.

After all, the advertisement can let people know about it. The branding makes a good association. They make the purchase, and then afterwards the product does a lot of the branding. Because if you buy that amazing hat and there is a hole in it when it comes, you might say "This product sucks, therefore brand sucks." Then you think the whole thing is a sham. Then you start to hate everyone who recommends it. Branding goes both ways.

**To grow a good brand:** Make promises and keep them, over and over again.

## Brand Pivots

When making changes, only two things can happen: you gain more than you lose, or you lose more than you gain. One-for-one swaps only happen on paper. You want to be net positive on all moves.

**Gaining more than you lose (good trade):** Small-town band hits it big. Some original fans say "they sold out." But topping charts, going on world tours, and earning a lifetime of financial security outweighs irritating a few locals. [CREATOR_NAME] went from rant videos in his closet to full productions about business. Some people only wanted the closet videos, but more business owners preferred better sound and video quality.

**Losing more than you gain (bad trade):** Band tries new music. More people hate it than like it. This is what happened to Pepsi with the Kendall Jenner ad.

**Reliable growth path:** Doing more of what already works, doing it better, showing it to more people. Lower risk.

**Higher risk path:** Change. When you make brand changes, you really make brand bets. You bet that you will gain more of a following than you lose, and the following you gain will feel strongly about what you sell.

**Bottom line:** Losing audience is a part of life. You just want to make sure you gain more than you lose. Do not let the five mean comments stop you from gaining the 500 new people who like the new thing.

**Accidental branding trap:** Most people's brands happen by accident. They take every podcast. They sell any customer. They talk about whatever is trending. And surprisingly to them, no one has any idea what they are about. Deliberate pairing is the fix.

**[CREATOR_NAME]'s own pivot:** When he started branding, he only made business content. As he got bigger he started touching on other topics. Views followed. But he realized it was the wrong views. So he recentered his content around only business. And unsurprisingly, more business owners paid attention and had positive associations with his brand again. Constantly monitoring how your audience responds to your content is important. It is how you keep a brand healthy and moving in the right direction on purpose, rather than by accident.

## Patience: The Final Step

Branding has higher returns than direct response advertising if you measure over a longer period of time. In the short term, if you just make associations, you might not make as much as making a crazy offer. But over time, how well you deliver, the people who talk about your brand, and the promises you make and keep, will be the things that build your reputation. They build what people associate you with.

There is a straightforward and reliable path to growth: doing more of what already works, doing it better, and showing it to more people. There is a higher-risk and possibly higher-reward path to growth through change. Constantly monitor how your audience responds to your content. That is how you keep a brand healthy and moving in the right direction on purpose, rather than by accident.

Follow this playbook and you will build a brand worth something. You will reap the benefits all the mega brands have. More clicks. More sales. Higher margins. More re-purchases. All for the lowest costs. And that is everything you need to make an enormously profitable business.

Having a strong brand is like a license to print money. You get higher response rates. Higher conversion rates. At higher prices. And people keep buying from you more. ## When Branding Fails (Compounding Damage)

- Ward off good customers
- Attract bad ones
- Pay more for customers than you should today
- Pay more for customers over time as negative word of mouth compounds faster than any other marketing (you will think you need a new marketing hack, but actually people who would have bought are now not buying because of what people are saying)
- Lose customers on the back end
- Need to keep running new crazy offers to get customers
- Always struggle to get good talent because real players do not want to work for you
- Constantly consider changing businesses

## The Hierarchy: Brand > Direct Response > Sales

"Those who can't brand, do direct response. Those who can't do direct response, sell. Those who can't sell, stay poor."

Branding sits at the top of the marketing hierarchy. It produces the highest returns over the longest time horizon. Direct response produces fast returns but requires constant reinvestment. Selling is the fallback. Each level up produces better economics.
