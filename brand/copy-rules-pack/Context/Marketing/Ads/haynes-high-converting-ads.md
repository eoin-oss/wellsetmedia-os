## Step 0: interrogate the value proposition until you hit the core care

Before a single hook, ask a pile of questions about the offer. Haynes ran this live on an agency selling "guaranteed 50 patients" to weight-loss clinics:

- What is the customer actually risking? ($3K retainer plus $3K ad spend. The customer does not split it. They see one $6K bet.)
- What is the outcome worth? (LTV $2,400 in person, $1,200 telemedicine.)
- How long until it is cash in hand? (6 to 12 months.)
- When is the customer profitable on the investment? (Month 1 to 2.)
- What proof exists? (Case studies profitable inside 30 to 60 days.)
- What else does the customer buy from the business, as a percentage?
- Are there sub-segments that need different hooks? (Telemedicine vs med spa: different cost base, different LTV.)

**"It depends" is not an answer.** It provides no certainty and cannot go in an ad. Force a number. If nobody knows, use the most conservative figure the owner would still agree is real, or below average.

**The core care** is the one thing the buyer gives a fuck about at the absolute centre, not the auxiliary benefits. For the B2B clinic offer it is "turn my $6K into more than $6K, as fast as possible". Haynes prefers "core care" over "mass desire" because it stops him getting distracted by side benefits.

**An offer can have more than one core care.** The B2C weight-loss version has two: the outcome (lose the weight) and safety (short and long term). Some people want the result but their skepticism about long-term effects overrides it. That is a core care, not a side objection.

**Separate core cares from short-sighted objections.** Fear of needles is real and common (the room was almost all "no" on liking needles), but it is an objection to overcome, not a core care to lead with.

**Watch the founder's own certainty.** Daniel claimed 30-day profitability but talked like he believed 60. Write to what the owner actually believes and can back, then use secondary benefits to shore up the timeline skepticism.

## The ad flow: four steps, every time

1. **Hook = the core care.** The hook should start with the core care. Weight-loss example: "Sustainable weight loss that is safe and medically supervised."
2. **Promise plus trust.** State the promise, then make it believable straight away with data, authority, mechanism. "These drugs help you feel full so you stop fighting your diet. You can trust them because the FDA trusts them. Here are the short and longer-term studies and the safety benchmarks they had to pass."
3. **Objections, layered.** Bounce through as many as you need, one after another. "Some people are afraid of needles. Here's a thin needle and a thick needle. Every semaglutide uses the thin one." Cut to a 10-second testimonial from someone who was scared of needles ("I thought a comfortable needle couldn't exist. A pinch hurts more."). Cut back. Next objection.
4. **Call to action.** Plain. "If you want to talk to a medical professional about getting started and get your questions answered, take this action and we'll get you started right away."

**Why step 3 matters most.** The objection step is what drives the conversion rate on the page itself. The ad's job is not to make the sale, it is to frame people so they take the next step at a high rate. If CTR is high but the page converts badly, the ad was a curiosity ad: it failed to answer what was on people's minds, so they went to the page looking for answers, didn't find them, and left. A page you have to overload with information is a sign the ad's hook, promise or objections were unclear or inaccurate.

**Pick the objections of the IDEAL buyer, not the wrong demographic.** Covering objections pulls in people far more likely to act, as long as they are the right objections. Cost is a good example:
- B2C, right buyer: "Most people think these drugs are expensive. The average is about $X a month. If that's too much, this probably isn't for you. For most people who come through us it's very reasonable."
- B2B, right buyer: do NOT pitch $6K as cheap. Answer the questions a buyer who can afford it asks: "There's typically a $6K to $10K upfront investment in ad spend and service fees. The question becomes how long it takes to get that back, and whether you can spend more sooner if it goes right."

**Medical, financial and other regulated claims:** support only what is provable (FDA approval, published studies, short-term safety data). Never claim long-term safety or outcomes you can't stand behind. Cross-check `Meta-Official/ad-creative-policy-standards.md`.

## Ad anatomy: what gets read, in what order

On a Facebook ad the eye goes **video/image first, headline second, primary text third.**

- **Video/image:** where the effort goes. It carries the full four-step flow.
- **Headline:** repeat the hook, the claim or the promise. Trust elements also work here, especially on image ads.
- **Primary text:** repeats what the video says, in the same four-step flow. Not a new message.

## Benchmarks and what retention is telling you

- **2% unique link CTR means the ad is doing a phenomenal job.** Aim for it. Haynes has very profitable clients at 0.5% to under 1%, so it is a target, not a requirement. Tai Lopez's "2%" is 6% (his relaunched 67 Steps ads; go and look at them in the ad library).
- **Thumb-stop / retention shape:** you want a high start with some room before it drops. A curve that drops off a cliff straight away means a poor opening hook. Every ad tails off at the end, which is normal.
- **People leave the moment they're sold.** Sold on the hook, they skip to the action. Sold on promise and trust, they leave and act. The people still watching the objection steps are the ones who aren't sold yet and want more information. Those segments still carry things the business wants every prospect to know before the call, which is why they get re-delivered in the hammer-them follow-up (six emails a day, etc.). Same logic for VSLs.

## The ad-idea brief (how Haynes hands ads to talent)

He types up 10 or so ad ideas per batch. Each one follows the four-step flow in brief: core-care hook, core promise with a data point for believability, one or more objections handled, CTA. Expect the talent to shoot about 9 of 10. That's fine.

For visual people, he adds **a list of reference videos to copy the style of**: camera angle, talking-head framing, editing, captions, environment. Copy the style, not the copy. Cody Sanchez's first batch without references came back rushed: bad lighting, a random environment, and the story told differently from the script. The batch with references came back right.

## Constants and variables: how to find the winning hook

**Same outro, different hooks.** When every ad targets roughly the same demographic (Jesse, prop-firm traders), Haynes changes the opening hook and core care per ad and repeats almost the same second half (promise reinforcement, objections, CTA) across all of them. If one ad wins, you know the hook is why.

Jesse example of a core-care hook plus reassurance: "Prop firms are the greatest opportunity for day traders, the best way to de-leverage the risk of losing your own money. That's why you started with them. Good thing you did, otherwise you'd have lost a lot more than you already have blowing accounts." Then the shared outro.

**When the audience is wide** (Cody: many different buyer types), the hooks appeal to different people so the outros can't all match. Vary the whole ad.

**Then iterate.** At launch you're guessing. The data shows how right or wrong you were. Drill into the winner with close variations, move away from losers, and **tell the talent exactly what lost so they never say it again.**

**Specific beats broad.** Brandon Carter (online fitness biz op), same campaign:
- Winner: "If you know about fitness, if you know about working out, you can make over $10K a month with your online fitness business." That's the core-care hook that has won repeatedly since 2019: you know fitness and nutrition, monetise it.
- Worst: "How much time are you going to waste on gimmicky get-rich-quick businesses? You need a proven, sustainable, future-proof business." Too broad. No fitness, no nutrition, no personal training, just anti-shiny-object.

**Once the hook is proven, vary the visuals:** how the person looks, the environment, wardrobe, camera distance, camera quality. Low quality can win. Tai filmed on a six-year-old iPhone on purpose after a lo-fi ad outperformed his RED-camera ads. Cody's rushed batch still turned $25K into $110K. Looking off-character can lose: Tom Cruise (the Section 8 real estate guy) with a neck beard performed terribly, because a polished Tom Cruise is what his audience expects.

## Character expectations: the foundation under everything else

> "Messaging is obviously extremely important. But if it's coming from the wrong character, it just doesn't land."

**The triangle.** All three have to match what the buyer expects:
1. **Environment.** Where you film.
2. **Look.** What you wear and how you look.
3. **Character traits.** How you talk, tone, directness, attitude.

Examples:
- Alex sold to lawyers in internet-marketer black tees. He switched to suits in his video pitches, on sales calls, and on his website and socials, and his response rate jumped. Lawyers trust people who dress like them. Nurses respond to scrubs.
- Haynes teaching people to make money: $41K watch, penthouse office, the cars. A Casio and a Camry would break the expectation.
- Capital raisers look almost identical: tucked-in polo or neutral button-down, dress pants, dress watches (not the flashy sport models), personal expression through socks only, serious and assertive tone. A Gucci tracksuit (Mike Sancho) pulls get-rich-quick people, not family offices.
- The Gushers-and-chamoy seller on TikTok: profane, slang-heavy, hood kitchen. That matched his buyers exactly, and the people in his comments were the same people.
- Tony Robbins, Grant Cardone, Tai Lopez and Gary Vee are all on-character. Gary is a "dynamic character" who reads right whether he's talking to the general public or to high-level people.

**Diagnostic:** if the wrong people keep coming through the funnel, there's a high chance the ads are playing the wrong character. Nobody tells you about a disconnect; you just see bad results.

**Be the real character.** Haynes isn't saying "be fake", he's saying play up the version of you that matches the buyer.

**Biz op in a burned market** (Amazon after the automation scams): don't swing to "regular guy" and don't flex materialism. Look professional, together, well-rounded, trustworthy, the opposite of the automation crowd. Use the burn as a hook: it's been proven that you can't have someone else do this for you, so you have to do it yourself, and those businesses claiming to serve thousands at once prove it doesn't take much time.

**Actors vs the owner:** the owner almost always wins when there's a personal brand. They're closer to the problems and have more word tracks. Actors need exact scripts. A founder dropping out of the creative is often a sign they're preparing to exit. UGC and actors are a fallback for owners who genuinely can't film.

## Language as a status signal

Same rule as "never name the qualifier", with the mechanism: describe circumstances, not demographic traits. Being direct about the trait creates skepticism. Describing the circumstance comes across as more sophisticated.

- Not "if you have a 750 credit score". Instead: "You go to the dealership, they approve you at a low rate every time. You're not getting burned on your mortgage. You've been set up financially for a while now."
- Pulling the well-off but aware (Amazon): "You've already got a good life. Nice dinners, you're not in the nosebleeds at the game, you drive a nice car, great job. This adds even more comfort and bandwidth to your income, and it doesn't take much time."
- **Word choice sorts people.** Someone doing well calls it their **career**, not a "9 to 5". "9 to 5" signals the lower tier. Use the vocabulary of the person you want.

## Reading ad performance through this lens

The flow that writes the ad also reads its results. Each step has its own metric, so a weak number points to the step that failed.

| Step | Metric that grades it |
|---|---|
| Hook (core care) | Thumb-stop / 3-second retention, early retention curve shape |
| Promise + trust | Link CTR (2% unique is phenomenal; 0.5 to 1% can still be profitable) |
| Objections | Landing page conversion rate |
| Character | Lead quality and show rate, i.e. whether the right people come through |

**Before analysing, tag every ad** with its core care, hook, the objections it covers, whether it uses the shared outro, and its visual variables (talent look, environment, camera). Results then roll up by core care and hook, not only by ad name. If the briefs were written per this doc, the tags already exist.

**IF / THEN:**
- **IF retention falls off a cliff in the first seconds** THEN the opening hook is weak (low thumb-stop). Rewrite the hook around the core care. Don't touch the body yet.
- **IF CTR is good but the page converts badly** THEN it's a curiosity ad: the objection step is missing or covers the wrong objections. Fix the ad first (add the questions prospects actually ask) rather than loading the page with more information.
- **IF CTR is under 2% but cost per result downstream is profitable** THEN leave it alone. 2% is a target, not a requirement. Judge on cost per qualified result.
- **IF the batch used a locked outro** THEN performance differences come from the hook. Rank hooks on cost per result AND volume, because dynamic creative pushes spend toward winners, so low-spend ads are partly the platform's verdict already. Next batch: close variations of the winner. Log the losers in the client's ad folder and tell the talent never to say them again.
- **IF the batch varied the whole ad** (wide audience) THEN you can't attribute a win to the hook. Read it as a whole-ad result, then re-test the winning hook against a locked outro.
- **IF the losing hooks are broad or abstract** (anti-shiny-object, generic "proven system") **and the winners are specific to the core care** THEN that's the Brandon Carter pattern. Retire the broad angle and drill into the specific one.
- **IF the same proven hook swings between versions** THEN the cause is visual: talent look, environment, wardrobe, camera quality. Compare those first. A polished look isn't automatically better (Tai's old-iPhone ads), but an off-character look tanks (the Tom Cruise neck beard).
- **IF leads are coming through but they're the wrong people** THEN check character (environment, look, tone) and language tier ("career" vs "9 to 5", circumstance vs demographic) before rewriting hooks.
- **IF the late segments get low completion** THEN that's normal, because people leave once they're sold. Don't kill an ad for completion rate. Make sure that information reaches prospects through the hammer-them follow-up instead.
- **IF prospects reach calls asking questions the ad should have answered** THEN add those as objections in the next batch.

For account-level roadblocks (fatigue, scaling ceiling, show rate, sales team), go to `haynes-if-this-then-that-diagnostics.md`. For the retention benchmarks (about 25% to 3 seconds, stickiness after 15 seconds), see `haynes-direct-response-mastery.md`.

## Writing checklist (apply to every ad)

1. Core care identified and written in one line. If the offer has two (outcome plus safety), note both.
2. No "it depends" anywhere. Every number is conservative and agreed by the owner.
3. Hook opens on the core care, framed as a circumstance, not a demographic.
4. Promise is followed immediately by proof or a data point.
5. At least 2 objections from the ideal buyer, handled one after another, with a short testimonial where one exists.
6. CTA is plain and says what happens next.
7. Headline repeats the hook or promise. Primary text mirrors the video's flow.
8. For a single-demographic batch: hooks vary, outro stays fixed. Mark the shared outro.
9. Brief includes a character note (environment, look, tone) and visual references for the talent.
10. Any regulated claim is provable. Policy pre-flight passed.

## Applying this at Elevate

- Every ad batch ships as an **ad-idea brief**: 8 to 10 ideas in the four-step flow plus a visual-reference list and a character note. Not loose hook lists.
- On a new client, run the **constants-and-variables** structure: 1 locked outro, 8 to 10 core-care hooks. Report which hook won, and log the losers in the client's ad folder so they never get rewritten.
- Run the Step 0 interrogation before writing for any client whose `knowledge.md` doesn't already answer: what the buyer risks, time to ROI, proof, and conservative numbers.
- Wrong-fit leads coming through? Check character (environment, look, tone) and the language tier before rewriting hooks. Then route through `haynes-if-this-then-that-diagnostics.md`.
