# Haynes Direct Response Advertising Mastery (CANON)

_Source: Jeremy Haynes, Inner Circle class "Direct Response Advertising Mastery", early January 2025. Transcript: `_sources/haynes-direct-response-mastery-2025-01-transcript.md`._

**What this is:** how to build the call-to-action ad itself. Direct response means any ad that tells someone to go do something and sends them to a conversion mechanism. This doc covers who the ad talks to, how it is structured, and how it is shot.

## Rule 0: every ad starts from the in-market demographic

This is the spine of the whole class, and Haynes says it is the single most common mistake that inflates cost and kills returns.

From Chet Holmes: at any moment only 3 to 4% of your total addressable market is **in-market**. They already identify as buyers. They want the thing now and they are looking for the right option. Everyone else is **needs-convinced**. They are real buyers, but you have to argue them into wanting it first.

- The in-market demographic is the highest ROAS, the cheapest to convert, and the most probable to buy. They show up to calls and webinars, they reply to DM ads.
- The needs-convinced demographic still makes money. It just makes less, at higher cost.
- **You always launch into the in-market demographic.** You only move to needs-convinced once you have genuinely exhausted in-market: every hook milked, every conversion mechanism used, and scale has forced you outward.

He demonstrated it live by polling the room: "who needs an executive assistant right now" got a handful of ones, "who could be convinced" got mostly twos. The twos cost more to convert. Go clean out the ones first.

### There is usually more than one in-market demographic

This is the leverage most people miss. Take the co-living real estate offer (rent rooms not houses, call the lease a housing club, nets 2K to 4K a month, buyer needs 70K to get in). The in-market pool is not just "people already in real estate":

1. Existing real estate investors who want more income without more properties.
2. People carrying a heavy tax burden who know real estate lowers it. Haynes uses himself here: 90K a month in tax, he would move on that instantly, and he does not identify as a real estate investor at all.
3. People already sold that real estate builds wealth who are just shopping for which vehicle (short-term, long-term, flips, land, commercial, Section 8, co-living).

Three separate in-market pools, three separate sets of hooks, one offer. Map these before writing a single hook.

### Never name the qualifier in the hook

Do not open with "if you make more than $100,000 a year" or "if you're an agency owner doing X". Haynes calls these surface level. They read as a red flag and put a barrier up immediately, and people scroll straight past.

Speak to the **circumstance** instead, which self-selects the same person without announcing the filter. Not "you make $100k and work a job", but "you're still in that job and not getting paid anywhere near what you want to make."

The reason you can afford to be indirect is pixel conditioning. You never let unqualified people report back to the pixel, so you never have to screen them in the copy. Screening in the copy is a symptom of a pixel that is being fed junk. See `haynes-demographic-spectrum.md`.

Worked example, the co-living hooks. Every one of them assumes the reader is already in real estate without ever asking:
- Looking to amplify your real estate income without adding new properties?
- Want to provide affordable housing solutions while boosting your returns?
- Ever thought about multiplying your rental income from a single property?
- Tired of the unpredictability of short-term rentals?
- Think managing multiple tenants is complicated?

The affordable-housing one is the sharpest. Nobody in real estate actually wants low-income tenants, they want the tax deduction and they are scared it will wreck their returns. That hook says "get the deduction and keep the returns" without saying either word.

## The ad flow: hook, reasons, call to action

That is the whole structure. It almost never varies.

### 1. Hook

Categories to pull from: pain, moving toward, moving away, motives (financial and otherwise), emotional drivers (anger, rage, insecurity, safety, lust, happiness), identity and characteristics, raw broad interests, mindset (what they are currently thinking about the thing), and circumstances.

**All hooks come down to context.** You want it to feel like you are talking to them in real time, right now. Not something they used to do, not something from a week ago.

**The 52,000 data points argument.** Facebook holds roughly 52,000 data points per user, updating live. Platforms trade data with each other constantly, and banks have had data-sharing contracts with ad channels for years (which is how income, net worth and liquid-asset targeting existed openly pre-2017). Haynes's point is not paranoia, it is that this is inventory you get to use. If someone just bought a meme coin, that transaction is known. An ad opening "I can't believe you're still out here buying meme coins, betting your net worth on Pepe and fart coin" is violently contextual to that person, whatever you pitch afterwards.

The feed proves it. Watch a video about Asian-market food hauls and the next unfamiliar creator you get served is doing the same thing. Haynes walked his own Instagram feed live: cars, watches, yachts, houses, wiener dogs, plus the people he had physically been with in the last three days. TikTok, YouTube and Facebook each show him a completely different diet because he uses them differently.

**Environmental congruence.** This is the second half of context and it is about what the ad looks like, not what it says. Your ad has to fit the feed it lands in. Tai Lopez's whole 2017-2018 run was three environments (the supercars, the Beverly Hills house, the watches) that matched exactly what his target was already scrolling past. The infamous "this house has a 20-car garage" opener is not wasted seconds, it is the ad blending into a feed full of cars and houses, plus affinity with the ad everyone already knows. Compare that with an ad that looks foreign and out of place in the feed. Looking foreign is one of the biggest reasons direct response ads fail.

### 2. Reasons (or story)

The middle is where most of the work happens. Reasons and story are the same thing, story just delivers the reasoning indirectly.

Requirements: believable, realistic, result-based where possible.

**The middle is the why.** People demand reasons. Haynes's example is how the public reacts to an atrocity: the frenzy runs until a motive is published, then it settles. "Why" is the first question asked when someone dies. The whole middle section exists to answer why this makes sense for you and why it makes sense for them.

**Pepper in several, do not lean on one.** Not one reason, a set: motives, justifications, emotional drivers, results, moving-toward and moving-away framing, all layered. Different reasons land with different people and you cannot know in advance which one lands.

Worked example, Brian Johnson: the hooks are "I'm trying to live forever", "I'm the most measured man on earth", "I spend $2 million a year measuring my body". The reasons are stacked continuously (before-and-after blood work, US life expectancy flatlining, the state of the food supply chain, why he sources his own olive oil and chocolate). Haynes pushed a member on this live: the member bought for the blood-work argument specifically, and openly rejects Johnson's no-sunlight position. Different reasons, same buyer. If Johnson ran only the no-sun argument he would sound like a vampire and lose that sale.

Supplements are the clean illustration of why-driven buying. Nobody takes them for no reason. They take them for a named outcome. That is why it works as a business model.

### 3. Call to action

Always simple. Tell them literally what to do next.

The strongest construction is a **"so you can" statement**: take X action so you can get Y result. "Press the button below so you can XYZ."

## Dynamic creative is the default

Haynes is a heavy proponent. A dynamic ad takes up to 10 images or videos, 5 headlines, and 5 bodies (Meta calls body copy "primary text"), and the platform recombines them per person.

- At $500/day or more, max every slot out.
- Under $500/day, run about half: 4 to 5 videos, 2 to 3 headlines, 2 to 3 bodies. Do not load in more variables than the budget can resolve.
- Test budget for a dynamic ad is normally $300 to $500/day. $100/day is the floor if budget is tight or it is a fringe test.
- **Always flick "optimize creative for each person" on.**

**Build the assets to be interchangeable, not matched.** The old way is to decide which video, headline and body belong together and force that combination. The modern way is to treat every variable as swappable with every other. You are trading "I demand you spend on this exact combination" for "give me the most standard events at the lowest cost per event".

**Split your variables deliberately:**
- Half long, half short. Two-minute videos and 30-second videos. Multi-paragraph bodies and one-sentence-plus-link bodies. Headlines that are a couple of words and headlines written for desktop width.
- Landscape and vertical, both.
- Multiple hook types across the set: attention-getters, circumstantial, interest-led, pain-led, mindset-led.

Tai Lopez running 12 different opening hooks against the same pitch, in both orientations, with caption variants, is the reference implementation.

This all rests on trusting the platform. You have 52,000 live data points working for you. Your job is to hand the algorithm enough raw material to combine.

## Majority, second and minority hooks, and foundational campaigns

Every market has a **majority hook**, which converts the most people, then a **second hook** that still performs well, then a tail of **minority hooks** that resonate with progressively smaller groups.

Cody Sanchez (buying businesses) as the worked example:
- Majority hook: a wave of baby boomers is retiring and will shut their businesses down, so use seller and creative financing to take over the cash flow and fund their retirement.
- Second hook: rather than starting a business, buy one instead.
- Minority hooks: narrower stories, for example earning equity in the coffee shop you already visit by doing their marketing.

**Every campaign has a ceiling.** Below it, ROAS is maximised. Push past it and ROAS drops away sharply. There is still money above the ceiling, but it costs far more to get.

So instead of scaling one campaign past its ceiling, you build **foundational campaigns**: a grid of campaigns, each one a hook crossed with a conversion mechanism, each parked below its own ceiling. For Cody that is webinar x hook 1, webinar x hook 2, webinar x minority hooks, then the same again for the VSL call funnel, plus "pop-ups" (small, topic-specific live webinars) which suit minority hooks best. You leave them running and they print.

When in-market is exhausted, you repeat the entire grid for needs-convinced: separate hooks, separate conversion mechanisms. They are two different ways of thinking and should not be mixed.

## Retention diagnostics

Pull the video engagement breakdown in Ads Manager. The shape is consistent across almost every ad.

- Impressions to 3-second views runs about 25%. **If more than 25% of people who see the ad reach 3 seconds, the hook is strong.** Do not read the initial drop-off as failure, it is normal.
- Roughly another third falls away between 3 and 15 seconds.
- **What actually matters is stickiness after 15 seconds.** Once past 15 seconds a good ad retains well through the halfway mark. Losing about half in the following 15 seconds after that is still fine.

The mechanism: people leave the instant they decide the thing is for them or not for them. Then they either act or they go. Nobody watches 100% of a VSL and expecting that is delusional. The job of the middle section is to hold the ones who have decided it is for them.

Reference point from the call: a live VSL campaign with 4 videos on dynamic creative at $61.13 cost per qualified call, 132,000 people to 3 seconds, 53,000 to 15 seconds, 46,000 to the halfway mark.

## Editing style is set by who you sell to

This is a hard fork, and Haynes calls it a critical differentiation.

**Selling to rich people:** sit down, set the camera up, talk. Be direct, look the part, sound the part, decent tonality. No rapid cuts. Rich people can endure a longer, calmer watch and rapid cutting actively repels them.

**Selling to the general public:** fast TikTok-style cutting works and will convert better. Scene changes every 2 to 3 seconds.

Do not copy an ad style from someone selling to a different tier. Haynes names Alex Hormozi's Skool ads as the trap: jumping out of boxes, cuts every three seconds, and an average student outcome around $1,300 a month. That is general-public creative. Modelling it while selling a $10K to $35K offer is a mistake. Cody Sanchez's floor is 10K with a new 35K offer, and Tai has moved fully into high ticket. Their ads are people sitting and talking.

**Set dressing counts.** Cody films against a chalkboard. She runs an education company. Haynes does not think that is an accident.

**Capital raising has its own template** (the Lux Locker example, which produced an investor who had sold a company to Barclays for $1.6bn):
- Always video, and the person must look the part. Big-money dress.
- Call out accredited investors explicitly.
- Lead with proof and cash-on-cash returns, and sell the vision.
- State the cheque size range out loud ("if you have between $50,000 and $900,000").
- Be extremely direct. Haynes has never seen an indirect capital-raise ad work.
- Under 45 seconds, then straight to the form.

## Ad fatigue and refresh cadence

- Cody's direct response creative refreshes roughly every 3 to 4 weeks.
- Tai holds ads for months, but he is an outlier on purpose. He optimises hard for click-through rate and he would rather sit through a downswing than swap. He treats a cost rise as a fluctuation that will revert.
- **For everyone else, treat it as fatigue, not a fluctuation.** Once result volume drops and cost per result climbs, the odds favour real fatigue that will not cross back. Refresh.
- Haynes's own framing: it is like holding a losing trade. Tai can hold it because he has infinitely more money. You cannot, so cut and re-enter.

## How to research: the ad library method

Go to the Facebook Ad Library, set the country you target, and search the page of anyone spending heavily, ideally a few hundred thousand a month or more. Not necessarily in your niche.

What to extract:
- How many ads they run at once.
- How many different opening hooks sit against the identical pitch, and which category each one is.
- Environments, landscape versus vertical, long versus short, caption variants.
- **Start dates.** The library shows how long each ad has run, which tells you what is actually working versus what was just launched.
- Who they sell to and at what price, so you know whether their style is even transferable to you.

Pro tip from the call: when an ad shows "this ad has multiple versions" it is running dynamic creative. Click "see ad details" to page through every video paired with it.

## Applying this at Elevate

- Before writing hooks for any client, list their in-market pools. Plural. If only one is listed the work is not finished.
- Kill any hook that opens by naming an income, a job title, or a business size. Rewrite to the circumstance.
- Ship hook sets, not hooks, and split them long/short and landscape/vertical so dynamic creative has real range. This reinforces `feedback_hook_variations_change_shape` (rotate the opener structure, do not reword the same one).
- Check the editing style against the buyer tier before approving a cut. Premium or high-ticket offers are not general-public offers.
- When an ad set slows, check the retention curve shape before touching anything else, then route through `haynes-if-this-then-that-diagnostics.md`.
