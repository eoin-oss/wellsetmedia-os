Part 1: The Foundations of High-Ticket B2B Ad Copy




Why B2B/High-Ticket Copy is Different


B2B and high-ticket advertising is a different ballgame. We are talking to an "in-market" buyer - someone actively seeking to solve their problem right now. 


This means our copy can and should be super direct.


Two Types of Offers:


A Commodity:


This is something already proven that the market actively wants and seeks (e.g. cold email, paid ads, email marketing). 


The entire market knows the benefit of this service, and a huge portion of them are actively looking for it.


If you sell a commodity, the in-market buyer wants exactly what you sell. 


This means you can script a ton of “extremely direct” ads and be pretty well off.

Think: “Do you want to start sending cold emails for your b2b business?”

Yes, that’s one of the best performing hooks for our client… $50 calls.


A New Opportunity: 


This is something the market doesn't know they want or need yet.


It's a new way of solving an old problem or a solution to a problem they don't realize they have. 


What makes something a "new opportunity" is that the market isn't necessarily actively looking for the physical thing you sell or associating the outcome/problem with your offer.


Don’t get this twisted - the person you are selling to is actively looking for the outcome or problem solved - they’re just not looking for YOUR deliverable.


If you sell a new opportunity, the in-market buyer is looking for the outcome you provide or the problem you solve, not your specific service.


So you can still be direct.


Where most people screw up when writing “outcome based messaging”:


They stay abstract for too long.


They talk about pain, confusion, inefficiency - but never tie it back to anything tangible.


When you’re selling a new opportunity, you can’t float in abstraction.


You have to plant the seed of your mechanism or solution early on, even if you’re going to explain it fully later…


For example:


“If your sales cycles keep stretching to 6 or 12 months, the problem isn’t your team - it’s that you don’t have the right assets to send prospects that speed up decisions…”


“It’s that you don’t have the right assets to send prospects that speed up decisions…”


This is the line which keeps things clear, and attracts high intent individuals.


The viewer immediately understands what kind of solution world they’re in (sales enablement content, video, etc)


They’re intrigued by how it works - but not lost in guessing what it is.


Because if you instead said:


“If your sales cycle is stretching out to 6, 8, or even 12 months, the problem isn't your sales team. The problem is your prospects are confused. And confused prospects don't buy”


It leaves too much for the imagination, and doesn’t hook in the right viewer straight away - the viewer actively seeking for a solution.


Because without teasing your solution, it can be 101 different things.


That’s the nuance most people miss…


Even if your mechanism (or solution) is a NEW way of doing things, you still need to make the link between the pain the prospect is experiencing and the tangible offer you sell early on.


Seed the mechanism / solution in the hook → then explain how it works in the body.


The Key Difference in Scripting:


All the structures and fundamentals stay the same for both, except for the THING that the prospect wants…


Commodity: 


They want YOUR SERVICE / THE INSTANT THING YOU SELL. If you sell cold email setup, the thing they want is cold emails. You can lead directly with the service.


New Opportunity:


They want THE OUTCOME. If you sell an AI agent that increases show rates, you have to link your solution to the outcome. They want higher show rates, so you talk about how AI agents increase show rates.


Important: 


You can still be direct with a new opportunity. For example: 


"Do your show rates suck? My 3 AI agents will do XYZ to fix that." 


You don't have to hide the offer before or during the education process.


You must always tie a SEED of an offer / solution early on to signal to the “in-market” buyer that this is for them…


While you can run less direct ads, a significant portion of your advertising must be direct to capture these active buyers.




Action Item


* Is your offer a commodity or a new opportunity?
   * What is the literal service you sell? Do people want this?


* What is the outcome they want?


* Write 2 ways to pitch your offer:
   * 1x direct to service version 
   * 1x outcome-led version 
	



The Cardinal Sins of Ad Scripting: Why Most Ads Fail


99% of ads fail for predictable reasons. Avoid these at all costs.


Sin #1: Sounding Smart vs. Being Understood


Trying to be cute or clever with your copy is a death sentence. 


Phrases like "it's not about X, it's about Y" or "that's not a business, that's chaos" mean nothing and do not move the sale forward.


Juxtaposition is incredibly strong, but only if used meaningfully and with actual purpose - when you’re actually shifting a belief or introducing new information.


Sounding smart is saying things that literally mean NOTHING and do not move the sale forward in any way, shape, or form. It's trying to be cute. 


You have to always think about WHAT ARE YOU TRYING TO SAY WITH EVERY SINGLE LINE.


9 out of 10 times, those cute little one-liners are either trying to do NOTHING, or they're trying to do something that can be DONE IN A MUCH BETTER WAY.


For example, "that's not a business, that's chaos" is still better than 99% of other shit out there, but it's still TERRIBLE.


Think: 


"What am I trying to say?”


“Okay, I'm trying to say their business is messy." 


“So how can I say it better?”


JUST DESCRIBE THE SITUATION AND SAY IT LIKE A HUMAN AND SAY IT CONVERSATIONALLY EXACTLY HOW THEY WOULD SAY IT.


With every single line you write, ask yourself: 


"What am I trying to say?" 


Every line must make someone instantly GET IT or instantly FEEL IT. It cannot be fluff or smart or cute. I do not want to think.


Sin #2: Missing the Mark on Problems & Outcomes


Most people write about problems how they think the prospect describes them, not how the prospect actually does.


Most ads fail 99% of the time because they don't say the situation, problems, or outcomes EXACTLY how the prospect thinks or describes it. 


Most people say stuff how they THINK the prospect says it.


You wouldn't say, "Are you struggling with creative fatigue?" even if that's technically correct.


A prospect CAN STILL KNOW it's creative fatigue, they just don't FEEL ANYTHING WHEN YOU SAY THAT. 


This isn't about awareness levels - it's about emotional resonance and how your words compute in the prospects head (no this sentence isn’t AI, this is just the only time this type of sentence makes sense)


Instead, you'd say: 


"You know that feeling... you find a winner in your ad account, you try to double the spend, and then everything just breaks. Your costs go up, conversions die, and terrible leads start appearing on your calendar out of nowhere." 


GO INTO DETAIL. Describe it how they would describe it.


Sin #3: The Unbelievable Promise


Prospects have a built-in BS detector. 


Most people focus too much on the "huge outcome" and not the "believable next step."


If you sell cold email and promise, "Send cold emails so you can add $500k in new revenue and exit your business," it goes in one ear and out the other. Not even necessarily because the prospect doesn’t ACTIVELY believe it, but because it's just WORDS and they don’t feel anything and their brain cannot physically link what you’re saying to that outcome.


Although it might be something they want, it just doesn't even go into the brain's computer because it doesn't feel believable or like a direct outcome of the service.


Focus on the believable next step. A better promise is: "...so you can start sending cold emails directly to prospects who want what you sell and actually have the money to pay for it." 


After you've established that believable outcome, you can then link it to the bigger dream if you want.


Sin #4: Sounding Like a Robot (or an Ad)


People are bombarded with ads all day. If your copy sounds like an ad, it gets ignored. 


People speak like an ad or like AI all the fucking time. Write like a human. Be conversational. Read your copy out loud. If it doesn't sound like something you'd say to a friend, rewrite it.


Sin #5: Being Vague and Indirect


One of the biggest reasons ads fail is being too vague, indirect, or trying to build intrigue. Intrigue is overrated. Clarity is king. Don't try to be mysterious. Be clear, be direct, and get to the point. Your prospects are busy; respect their time. You can be clear AND direct. Use intrigue only if it’s paired with a clear signal of relevance, outcome, or solution. Never use pure curiosity.




Context


Context is critical for many reasons. Firstly, you must assume someone hits your ad with no context on who you are and what you do. You have to fill that gap in their brain at some stage.


AI often has an issue where it writes like it assumes everyone has the knowledge you fed it. That is not the case. Your prospect is scrolling through their feed, sees your ad, and has zero idea who you are, what you do, or why they should care.


This is why you must:


1. Clearly state what you do (or what problem you solve) early in the ad.


2. Provide enough context so they understand the relevance of your message.


3. Back up every claim with proof (more on this in the next section).




The Power of Proof


PROOF is massively, massively important. There are many types of proof, and some form of proof must be in every single ad. Every claim MUST be backed up by some proof.


Types of Proof:


1. Logic: Explain the reasoning behind your claim in a way that makes sense. "If you do X, then Y happens because of Z." This is especially powerful when combined with situational articulation. Or my personal favourite “think about it…”


2. Results: Show the outcomes you've achieved for clients. Numbers, percentages, revenue increases, cost decreases - anything tangible.


3. Case Studies: Tell a story of a specific client who had a problem, used your solution, and got a result. This is proof + storytelling.


4. Implied Authority: Demonstrate your expertise through the depth of your knowledge, the specificity of your language, or by calling out things only an insider would know. When you articulate their exact situation in detail, you build implied authority.


5. Quick fire authority: Sentences like “just like we’ve done for [x] other [icp’s] over the past [y] years.
6. Mechanism articulation / explanation: If you can clearly explain how you solve the problem and it makes complete 100% sense, especially after clearly stating their exact situation - that is PROOF that you can solve the problem.


Why Proof Matters:


Without proof, your claims are empty. With proof, your claims become believable. Believability is the bridge between interest and action. If they don't believe you can deliver, they won't click.




Action item


* Make a list of 3 believable, non-fluffy proof lines you can drop in any ad


* These can be:
   * A client result or % lift
   * A relevant name-drop
   * A quick logic line that makes the offer make sense
   * A mini-case study or transformation


* Keep these short - you’ll use them in almost every ad.
	







Part 2: "Andromeda" & Creative Strategy




Creative Diversity Under Andromeda


We know with Andromeda that creative diversity is important.


It’s important in the ad delivery process - however, basic conversion principles stay the same.


If you’re writing ads that sound like a robot, aren’t talking about real pains, desires, etc


And you aren’t articulating things in a clear way, you still won’t get any conversions


Andromeda and diversified ads is the outer coating of the ad, you still need good copy and ideas for those ads to actually convert.


The Power of Visual Hooks


We recently did an account sweep across our dozens of clients and $1.8M in ad spend per month and found a common thread.


The ads that performed, had a fantastic hook rate. But not just this… they had an AMAZING “hold rate”


Hook rate = % of people that watched the first 3 seconds


Hold rate = the % of people who watched the first 3 seconds that continued to watch 50%


We also found that ads with a great hook rate, but bad hold rate… sucked


So it’s important that your hook doesn’t “bait”  everyone


But ATTRACTS the right viewer


A great way to do this is with some visuals in the hook…


This also leans into the Andromeda update


The Andromeda update also means we need massively unique visuals for each ad. Tonality, how they look, the movements they do - the first 3 seconds are the most important. 


Make sure there is something different happening in the first 3 seconds of each ad.


A visual hook is essential


These don't have to be massively hard to do or crazy. It can be something small:


* A camera angle change
* Throwing something in the air
* A hand gesture
* A whiteboard in the background
* Pulling up a piece of paper
* Using a prop
* Standing up
* Sitting down




The 30-30-30-10 Rule for Creative Diversification


In all of your diversified ad sets, this is an easy rule to follow (not ridgid)


* 30% Direct: Ads that call out the offer or the core problem directly.

* 30% Proof & Authority-Led: Ads that lead with case studies, testimonials, or social proof.

* 30% Creative/"Needs Convinced" Angles: Ads that use more creative hooks, tackle false beliefs, or educate the prospect.

* 10% Wild & long form: Go for a hail mary, something completely different and fun you want to test






Part 3: The Core of Your Ad: Offer, Angles, and Ideas






Breaking Down Your Offer (So You Can Actually Write About It)


Most people overcomplicate their offer. They can't explain what they do in a clear, simple way. And if YOU can't explain it clearly, your prospect sure as hell won't understand it.


Before you write a single ad, you need to break your offer down into 4 simple parts. This does two things:


   1. It helps you write clear, ascending copy - you'll know exactly how to take someone from "here's what I sell" to "here's the outcome you want" in a logical flow


   2. It gives you different starting points for your hooks - each part becomes a different ad angle


Here's how to break it down:


The 4 Parts of Your Offer:


Part
	What is it
	Description
	1
	The literal, tangible service, or deliverables
	Cold email setup offer
	2
	Direct outcome
	Start sending cold emails to ideal prospects
	3
	Benefits of outcome
	Book consistent qualified sales calls
	4
	Your Differentiators
	One time payment, AI personalization
	



Why This Matters:


Once you have these 4 parts clearly defined, you can write copy that ascends logically from one part to the next.


Here's an example using all 4 parts:


"Pay me once (differentiator) and I'll set up an entire cold email system into your business (what you sell), so you can start sending thousands of emails directly to prospects who want what you sell and have the money to pay for it (direct outcome). That way you can book between 10-15 sales calls per month (benefits of outcome). We combine mass AI personalization at scale (differentiator/mechanism) so that you only say the right thing to the right person at the right time"


See how it flows?


You're not jumping around. You're taking them on a clear path from what you do → what they get.


And it makes it significantly easier!


BONUS Tip:


The closer you can get to selling the literal service & deliverables - the higher intent buyers you will attract. 


The higher quality buyers you will attract.


For example, one of our best performing hooks for a client was this:

“Are you looking to start sending cold emails for your b2b business?”


It attracted the most volume, the highest intent, and the highest quality.


Notice how we didn’t lead with


“Are you looking to book between 10-20 meetings per month”


No - we sell cold email - we attract people who want cold email.


(This is the power of having a “commodity” as your offer)




Generating Proven Angles (Without Inventing Fake Ones)


Again with Andromeda, creative "diversity" is the name of the game. This doesn't mean we have to invent entire new angles for people to buy that just don't exist.


The conversion principles must stay the same - that you are speaking to real-time contextual pains and desires of your market that are felt across the whole market. Inventing new angles might help with the delivery process on the Andromeda level, but still no one is going to convert.


You have to take the proven angles and common "reasons to buy" and package them differently. 


Say the hooks in a wildly different way - with the same messaging, benefits, undertones, and desires baked in. Same with the body - but try a different copywriting structure.


Example:


Let's say the idea is for a cold email agency and it's that you want predictable leads.


In one ad we can say:


"Do you want predictable leads for your business?"


In another diversified ad we can say:


"Are you fed up with not knowing where your leads are coming from?"


These are still proven angles and reasons for people to buy but spoken drastically different. 


Accompany that with different visuals and backgrounds and Meta knows none the wiser.

Writing In-Market Direct Ads


When writing direct ads for in-market buyers, you are not educating them on the model, the problem, or even the category.


You are only educating to shift a belief or introduce a new lens, not to explain your category or service from scratch.


They’re already “in the game.”


That means they either:


Know their problem and want it solved.


Are already looking for a provider like you.


Are actively buying or comparing options.


What This Means:


Your job is NOT to convince.


Your job is to clearly show that:


You understand their current situation.


You’ve solved this exact problem before.


You have a better, more believable way to get the result.


You’re easy to say yes to.


Writing "Needs Convinced" / Idea-Led Ads


There’s a second category of buyer that isn’t cold - but they’re not itching to buy yet.


They might be skeptical. They might have tried a bunch of things. They might be burnt out or simply confused by the noise.


This is where “idea-led” ads shine.


This is also where most “new opportunity” buyers lay.


You come in through the side door - not with a direct offer, but with a new lens, a grounded insight, or a truth bomb that makes them stop and think.


You’re seeding a new belief that gets them to say:


“Shit… maybe this is the missing piece.”


Structure of a Great Idea-Led Ad:


   * Start with a bold but grounded insight (pattern break or belief shift).
   * Unpack that idea in a way that feels honest, intelligent, and direct.
   * Show how that idea relates to their situation.
   * Then transition into the offer with: “That’s why we built X…”


The Anatomy of a High-Converting Ad


Every ad has three parts:


   * Hook: The first 5-30 seconds that grab attention.
   * Body: The main part of the ad where you deliver your sales argument & offer.
   * CTA: A recap of what they want, and what you want them to do next.




Creating 15 Different Ideas


Different ad ideas are simply different avatars.


What set of words or phrases make a specific avatar more likely to convert.

Rather than thinking about “ideas” - think about people.


Different people in your market.


99% of anyone who buys your product will care about 1 or 2 core outcomes / results. 


Examples of core outcomes: 


“Buy an off-market multifamily apartment that pays you every month”


“Start sending cold emails that book calls your your b2b business”


“Run profitable paid ads that book calls profitably for your business”


“Having a high converting funnel that converts cold traffic”


But every single person is in a different situation or has a different set of stimuli that they respond to.


Some people respond to emotion more.


Some people respond to logic more.


Some people respond to FOMO more.


Some people care more about saving time.


Some people have been experiencing a specific situation.


Some people care more about saving money.


Ideas are about speaking to these different people in your market.


All who have the same goal, want the same deliverables, and want the same outcome but just have different triggers, buying motivations, and reasons to act now.


The majority of your ad ideas will be tied to an outcome or pain removal - they’re the easiest and quickest to spin up.


Here are a few examples for a funnel building agency:


   * Idea 1: Someone who has a brand new product launch coming up soon and needs a funnel ready to convert out the get go without guessing or burning money

The outcome is the same:


Having a high converting funnel that converts cold traffic.


Just different buying triggers. 


Different problems you’re entering their mind with. 


Different situations they’re experiencing.


So therefore your landing page is still congruent. Your VSL is still congruent.


Because it’s still focused on the outcome or main problem.


But your ads are talking about situations.


The important thing too is that different ICP’s in your market can experience all of these things, it’s just which is most pressing at the time of seeing your ad.


Rather than situations, there are also “opportunity or grand narrative” ideas.

Hooks


The hook is by far… not even close.


The most important, impactful part of your entire ad and 80% of your time should be spent on the hooks…


Because the hook of your ad is one of the ONLY “guarantees” in your entire funnel.


What I mean by this is that if someone goes through your funnel… they HAVE to at least watch the hook of your ad in order to physically click through to the landing page (because the button won’t pop up)


So it’s a GUARANTEE within your funnel.


If you layer in enough desire, benefit, clarity, and authority - many times some people JUST watch the hook and click through and take action.


On top of this (the more obvious use of a hook) is to make someone continue watching your ad.

Tactic: A Winning Hook Checklist


               * Audience. 
               * Benefit. 
               * Curiosity. 
               * Offer / Solution Tease.


Audience


Your hook must immediately be clear who this is for - it’s a non negotiable. Usually this comes in the form of a “callout”.


A “callout” isn’t necessarily: “coaches, consultants, & agency owners”


A “callout” is ANYTHING that helps the viewer understand that this ad is for them…


There’s an important layer when it comes to callouts - the more specific your callout or the PERCEIVED specificity… the more it will land with your ICP.


If you say something like

“If you’re a coach, consultant or course creator at $20k/mo in revenue, listen to this”


It lands much less than:


“If every time you scale your ad spend, your cost per call skyrockets, lead quality plummets, and just random people who would never buy start coming through your funnel, listen to this”


You can get away with direct callouts - but it is always important to remember “perceived specificity” - if I FEEL like you are talking directly to me… I am more likely to pay attention.


Here are a few examples of different callouts you can use.


Direct callout = “If you’re a coach at $20k/mo in revenue”


Problem callout = “If you’re a coach struggling with getting leads”


Desire callout = “If you’re a coach who wants to get more leads”


Situation callout = “If every time you scale your ad spend, your cost per call skyrockets, lead quality plummets, and just random people who would never buy start coming through your funnel, listen to this”


Benefit 


Your hooks absolutely must imply some sort of benefit or “what’s in it for me” for the viewer.


Benefits can mainly come in 2 ways.


Direct benefit = “...then I will help you book 20-30 calls per month”


You are telling them exactly what you are going to help them do or what problem you are going to help them solve… a super effective way to do this.


Is to plant a “seed of your offer”


This is basically implying that you are going to give them an offer in the ad (this attracts high intent individuals who want their shit solved NOW)


This usually sounds like:


“...Then we will help you get [benefit]”


Implied benefit = “...then listen to this”


This is similar to planting a seed of your offer - but here it’s more of an IMPLIED benefit - it’s clear that you are going to help in some way… but you haven’t directly said it


Curiosity


Curiosity in the traditional sense isn’t always necessary.


Most people think curiosity is WITHHOLDING information to get someone to watch more… this is not how we use curiosity.


We layer it into the rest of the hook.


It’s things like “listen to this”


“Let me show you how we can help you get xyz”


Or sometimes, you can layer in old school curiosity… but this is one of those things where it’s not necessary. But can improve the impact of your hooks, as curiosity can get people to watch more.


And a good hook must be conversational. It’s not quippy or smart.


If you’re a B2B SaaS founder [audience] who wants to start sending cold emails [benefit], listen to this… [curiosity]


99% of all hooks will follow this structure in one way or another.


If every time you scale ad spend, ROAS drops and random unqualified leads start finding themselves onto your calendar [audience]... listen to this [curiosity] 


(The benefit is implied that because you’ve described their situation exactly, so they assume you’re going to solve it)


Every single hook. Every single one. Should plant a “seed” of a solution or offer in it. Even curiosity must signal the category or outcome.”


“Listen to this”


“Let me show you how we can help you get [result]”


“Then listen to how we can [remove problem]”


This signals to the people actively looking for results, pain removal, or a deliverable that this ad is for them.


The Hook IS The Idea 


Like I mentioned before… the hook is THE most important part of the entire ad.


But a great mental model to understand how to write great hooks is thinking of it through the lens that the hook IS the idea…


That gives you more room to play around… because you’re no longer trying to bait intrigue or bait them in - you’re trying to get across the whole idea of your ad.


Most people treat hooks like cheap bait.


A one-liner to grab attention. A pattern interrupt. A flashy start.


But that’s wrong.


A high-performing hook is the strategic idea behind the entire ad - condensed into as many sentences as you need to hammer it home.


It’s the core angle.


The thing you really want to say - just said up front.


It sets the tone, audience, problem, outcome, and even the offer… all in a few seconds.


Example:


“If you’re running ads for your high-ticket service or info product and they’re just not working - you’re getting $200, $300, even $400 cost per calls, those calls barely show up, and when they do they’re just not qualified in any way shape or form. Listen to this.”


This is the idea.


               * There is no separation between the hook and the core ad concept.
               * It directly identifies a specific audience.
               * It describes their real-world situation in detail - how they would describe it.
               * It implies the outcome (you’re going to fix this).
               * And it tees up the solution without needing fluff, curiosity, or filler.

Bridge the gap between the hook and the action you want them to take - clearly, logically, and convincingly.

Easy Body Frameworks




Direct Body - Clear Offer
                  * Give the offer
                  * (What they get, who it’s for) make sure you speak directly to them and what you're going to do for them, start with "Here's my offer to you" - no fluff straight up and direct and clear.


                  * So that you can (tie it to a desire)
                  * Straight up write “so that you can [desire/benefit/outcome]” this has to be a quantifiable outcome that the prospect can link to your service. It can’t be abstract or far detached from what you do. For example if you’re selling cold email, you can’t say: “So that you can finally buy your Mum a house and your Dad will finally be proud of you” - it would be more fitting to say “So that you can have qualified sales appointments book themselves directly onto your calendar without any work or effort on your end”


                  * Expanded benefits
                  * Here you say: “That way you can…” again, it has to feel close to the thing you do. Sticking with the cold email example it would be - “That way you can focus on revenue driving activities and not waste time sending cold DM’s”


                  * (Optional) Belief break (why they haven’t solved it yet)
                  * This is where you let them know why what they’ve tried before (or thought of trying) hasn’t (or won’t) work. Usually it reads like “most people think they way to get [result] is by [common solution]. Or “most people think the way to fix [problem] is to [common solution] - the key here is it actually has to be something common and it has to be believable that its not the real solution. If I said “most people think the way to scale your business is to run ads, but it’s actually through knocking on doors” - I wouldn’t believe you and I’d think you’re full of shit.


                  * Mechanism (how this works differently)
                  * This is where you introduce the main mechanism/how you get the outcome or result. This doesn’t necessarily mean the “double backflip 42” - it can just be simply explaining how you get the result. For example: “The way to actually run profitable paid ads is by having a complete system that doesn’t just book in calls at low costs, but a back-end selling system that warms up the prospects before sales calls”


                  * Proof/authority
                  * Straight after you explain the mechanism (and link it to the result/outcome) you have to give some quick proof/implied authority - we’re not going in depth with cases studies here. It’s just a few (or even one) simple line letting the prospect know you’ve done this before: “Just like we’ve done for john who got y result” or “Just like we’ve done for [x] other business owners across [y] other industries”


                  * (Optional) Big opportunity or grand narrative
                  * This is optional but really strong if you have one - for example lets say you sell a biz op on crypto, a “bull-run” is your big opportunity/grand narrative. It’s what's making your opportunity even stronger. If you sell “trust based advertising” your big opportunity is the fact that we’re in the lowest trust environment ever. This section is the “cherry on the top” of your mechanism and the reason why its working well RIGHT NOW.


                  * Objection handling
                  * This is where you address the main objection at this stage in a simple clear and non salesy way - if you’re trying to get someone to opt into a webinar a line that would help is: “It’s live, it's free, its only 90 minutes, and its starting soon”


                  * CTA (book a call, watch VSL, etc.)
                  * Your CTA has to be a strong closing line and has to be extremely clear. You absolutely cannot do a “if that sounds good click the button below”. You have to recap the entire ad in about 5-10 seconds with a CTA. Usually it looks like this: “So if you’re finally ready to get [result/benefit] and you want to stop [pain] and you want [deliverables]... click the “learn more” button below, watch the video on the next page, then book in a free call with me. On the call we’ll run through your business, what’s working, what’s not and how we can help you.

Tips for Writing Better Bodies 


                        * Write how they talk. Say it the way they would explain it to a friend.


                        * Describe situations, not labels. Don’t say “you’re burned out.” Say: “You open your calendar and see 4 no-show calls, a Stripe refund, and 20 Slack messages from your team asking if they should pause ads again.”


                        * Inject mechanism subtly. You DON'T need to say “our unique mechanism is…” Just explain what you do clearly, and why that works when others don’t.


                        * Use proof as argument. Add small, concrete examples or client stories to back your claims naturally.


                        * Drive to CTA smoothly. End your body with a line that tees up your offer like “So here’s what we’ve built…” or “That’s why we created…”




Stay Rooted in The Rule of One


Every body section must be built around ONE core idea or angle.


If your hook is about fixing show-up rates, don’t pivot mid-body into talk about long-term client retention. That breaks the through-line and kills the sale.


Every section of your body should work together to advance one central, emotionally resonant idea. When in doubt, ask:


“What is this ad really trying to say?”
“How can I make that idea hit harder, feel realer, or be clearer?”




Using The False Mechanism / False Solution Throughout


A false solution is the thing your audience has already tried or believes will work - but doesn’t.


A false mechanism is the reason most solutions fail - because they're based on the wrong logic or premise.


Using false solutions and mechanisms does three powerful things:


Creates contrast and sharpens your offer’s uniqueness


You’re not just “another option.” You’re the only option that actually makes sense.


Builds trust by validating past frustrations


“You’re not dumb. You just bought into what everyone else told you would work.”


Raises stakes emotionally


“Not only do those old ways not work - they’re probably why you're stuck right now.”


You must explain WHY the current solutions don’t work before you present the real one.


Human Ways To Inject This Messaging


Use these naturally throughout your copy to seed the contrast without sounding “salesy”:


                        * “You’ve probably tried ___”
                        * “Here’s what most people don’t realize…”
                        * “Everyone teaches you to ___ - but that’s exactly why ___”
                        * “At first, we thought the problem was ___ - until we discovered ___”
                        * “Sounds smart on paper, but in reality…”


The "Offer" Section & The "Mechanism"


In every ad, there is a point where you transition into the offer. This has to be extremely clear, direct, and compelling. We can take our time here; the bridge has to feel natural.


Example transition: "So here's what I want to do for you…"


This is a clear transition from whatever we were speaking about before. Everything must be conversational.


We must talk about the clear reasons that the offer is different, what type of offer it is, and exactly what you're going to do for the ICP and how it links back to the result they want.


Talk about the mechanism in which you get the result or the unique selling point and benefits of that in the offer section.


If it's a setup offer, talk about the one-time payment, and how much you're going to do for them, and the fact that there's no retainers, no locked-in contracts - they pay once and get everything.


Going back to the 4 steps of your offer, use this to help write your offer - don’t be vague.

And it makes it significantly easier!


What is a "Mechanism"?


"Mechanisms" aren't necessarily (or ever) your "proprietary reverse wormhole 42 strategy"


The entire point of a mechanism is for the prospect to clearly see and understand how you get the result. 


That is all.


If your mechanism does that, it's a good mechanism. 


It can be as simple as "we will help you get X by doing Y."


If it makes the result feel believable, and tangible, and I can think "yeah, makes sense" - that's a mechanism…


A good mechanism builds BELIEF & PROOF that you can get the result (or they can get the result) 


It is not to make you "unique" or “standout” it is to simply convey competence and a clear, easy-to-understand way to get the result - if it’s unique, great.


In a market where EVERYONE has case studies, testimonials, social proof - a SICK as fuck mechanism can build more BELIEF & PROOF than any amount of case studies.

Writing to non broke people


Most of the people you’re writing for already know what they want.


They’re in-market. They’ve got a business. They’ve got traction. They’ve got money.


Your job isn’t to convince them of the dream.


Your job is to speak directly to the thing they already want - and do it better, faster, clearer than everyone else.


That means no fluff.


No clever analogies.


No “sell the hole, not the drill” BS


If someone’s already walking into Home Depot to buy a drill, just sell them the fking drill.


They already know they need it.


They’re ready to buy.


They just need clarity.


Same with your ads.


If you’re selling landing pages, don’t do some vague “do you want to scale your brand?” 


Just say:


“Do you need a new landing page because you’ve got a product launch coming up?”


That line works because it’s talking to someone who is in motion.


They’ve already decided they need help.


They’re looking for someone who actually gets it.


The same applies for any high-ticket B2B offer - outbound, email, capital raising, whatever.


Don’t go after broke people who need a ton of convincing.


Go after the buyers who are already paying for the outcome - and just want a better way.


Your goal isn’t to educate them from scratch.


Your goal is to get in front of them at the right time with a message that says:


“I know what you need. I do that. Let’s talk.”


So when you’re writing for buyers who are not broke, write like this:


                        * Assume they’ve tried stuff before
                        * Use the words “You already know…”
                        * Be literal, not abstract
                        * Be direct
                        * Prioritize proof
                        * Pitch them like they’re capable, not clueless


Part 5: The Polish: Refining Your Copy


Situation Articulation


The only way your copy will resonate is if you articulate the specific situation of the prospect in a way that they themselves describe.


No AI slop. Not how the "technically right way to say it is."
Bad example:


"Are you struggling with the creative scaling trap where you can't increase spend?"


Good example:


"Look, you get what I mean, man. You have some ads, they're performing great - getting good cost per calls, you've even gotten a few deals from them… but when you try to scale your spend, and scale that campaign to that $500 a day mark, shit just stops working. Costs go up, conversion goes down, and terrible terrible leads start appearing on your calendar seemingly out of nowhere."


Avoiding Empty Claims


When writing ads you have to be extremely cautious of "empty claims" - AI is a huge culprit of this.


Example of an empty claim:


"You don't have an ads problem, you have a systems problem."


These are empty claims. There is no evidence, proof, or reason for this being said. It's empty space and it's just you trying to be cute or look smart.


This tactic is juxtaposition or "breaking a belief" - but in order for this to be effective, you have to hold a position of authority. There has to be proof, and you have to tee this up.


You do this by either:


                        1. Articulating the problem or situation of the ICP in such immense clear detail that they fully 100% believe that you get their exact situation, so if you introduce a new way of thinking or a claim it lands.


                        2. Giving a mass amount of proof, logic, and authority for the exact same reason.


Even then, just think: "What am I trying to do with this sentence?"


If the answer is "break the belief" - you can't do that with one sentence. It has to be longer, more explanatory, and make logical sense.


Every line of your copy has to make someone instantly GET IT or INSTANTLY FEEL IT. It cannot be fluff or smart or cute. I do not want to think.
