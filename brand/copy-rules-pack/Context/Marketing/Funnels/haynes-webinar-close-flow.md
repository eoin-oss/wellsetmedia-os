## 1. Diagnose before you touch the close

Two separate gates. Never fix the wrong one.

| Metric | Benchmark | If below |
|---|---|---|
| Retention, peak show rate to start of pitch | 80%+ | The presentation is the problem. Fix content before the close. |
| Book-a-call rate, of people present at pitch | 30% (not 20; 20 is routinely beaten) | Retention good + booking bad = fix the close, not the webinar. |
| Direct-to-checkout conversion, of people present at pitch | 10% | Same: fix the close. |

- Direct-to-checkout works up to $5K; converts best at $3.5K or less. Book-a-call: 30% regardless of price.
- A steep drop DURING the pitch marks the exact slide that is wrong. Correlate the recording with the retention graph, go to that timestamp, change it, even if you think it is good.
- Shape: ~90 min total, pitch starts around minute 60, close runs ~30 min. Q&A is the variable that stretches it.

## 2. The close sequence (order matters)

1. **Ask for the close.** Bridge out of the presentation by asking permission: "Drop a 1 in the chat if it's cool that I share how I can help you beyond today." Chat floods with 1s, you transition with zero awkward slides. (Fladlien's "permission to close" announces the pitch in the first 60 seconds of the webinar; Haynes's ask happens at the transition itself. Use both.)
2. **Pre-frame objections, indirectly.** Before any offer slide. Take every known objection from sales calls and frame how to think about it without naming it as an objection. His examples: price shock as identity ("what version of you exists on the other side of buying this"), fear as residue from earlier decisions (the bell-ringing frame), Hermes costly-vs-expensive, the Richard Mille/deadmau5 lesson (an unframed offer gets roasted; framing determines how the object is seen). These pre-frames get ANCHORED back to later, in objection handling: "remember what I said about costly versus expensive." Anchor, do not re-expand.
3. **Outcome slides, one per value-stack item.** Never dump the stack cold. For each: the outcome they want, the stack item that delivers it, use-case slides showing it in practice, contextual testimonial screenshots for that specific item. Ask "tell me in the chat if you want this outcome" as you go.
4. **Full value-stack recap slide.** Only after every item has been paired to an outcome.
5. **Price, pogo-stick.** Say it and bounce off it. Never dwell, never cliffhang. Drop it right after the stack recap.
6. **Clear CTA.**
7. **Who it's for / who it's not for.** Directly after the CTA. Reaffirms buyers, dispels the unqualified.
8. **CTA again.**
9. **Objection handling or Q&A.** Same slot, same function; Q&A is objection handling freestyled. This is where you anchor back to the pre-frames. Plant common objection questions in the chat via mods if needed.
10. **Repitch from a different angle.** Never repeat the first pitch. Rotate the object: describe the same offer from a side the first pitch never showed (his example: inner circle pitched first as access/training/community, repitched as the room where rich, isolated people de-risk big financial bets together). Every offer has a second side. Find it before the webinar, script it.
11. **Risk reversals.** See §3.
12. **Repitch again, close with Q&A, then a final repitch.** That is the full flow.

## 3. Risk reversal is a buying frame, not a guarantee

Objections are hesitations (money, timing, partner). Risks are exposure ("you're asking for $120K over a year; what happens if it goes wrong?"). Handle them separately.

- Guarantee/refund slides belong here if you have them, but they are the floor, not the technique.
- The real move is reframing the frame the decision is made through:
  - **"You don't need confidence in us, you need confidence in you."** We're proven (testimonial run, including how failures were handled: kicked out, refunded). The variable is you. Anchor back to who-it's-for.
  - **Urgency via inevitability** (the Jim Rohn seasons frame): winter always comes; if it is spring for you, act now with urgency. Frame the decision inside a belief the prospect already holds.
- Talking honestly about refund rate, dispute rate, and what you do when things go wrong is itself a risk reversal.

## 4. Testimonial placement

- Contextual, everywhere, throughout the close: any slide (outcome, objection, risk) gets a testimonial screenshot specific to the thing being discussed.
- Screenshots while you keep talking. No testimonial videos: they break the energy and the buying frame.

## 5. Pricing rules

- **Never pitch cheaper than what you actually want to sell.** No downsell-first. Pitch the real offer; if the audience can't be one-to-many closed on it, push to book-a-call instead, and financially model the added friction (show rate, close rate) before committing.
- **Say the price?** Direct-to-checkout: always. Book-a-call: saying it pre-qualifies (treat the webinar as a one-to-many sale in progress); omitting it buys call volume for price-fearful general-public audiences where the phone walks them through funding. He biases toward dropping price; successful webinars split ~50/50. This is consistent with the demographic-spectrum rule: never price-transparent in a call funnel for lower pockets.
- **Two price points (PIF vs plan, or core vs premium): the onion pitch.** Pitch one, peel back to the next. Multiple ways in, stated in sequence, no apology.
- **Friction is a capacity tool.** Deposits, applications, hidden pricing: add friction only when you cannot handle the volume. If you can handle the volume, remove the friction.

## 6. Production discipline

- **Dry-run like game footage.** Record yourself presenting the finished deck before the live run, watch it back, cut what you over-elaborated, fix awkward transitions. Do the slide math first: slides x seconds-per-slide / 60 = runtime.
- **Sales team runs regardless of outcome.** Setters hit registrants who didn't show. Closers hit people who stayed to the close but didn't book or buy. Reps who sent a prospect to the webinar that then bought get a cut, or they stop sending people.
