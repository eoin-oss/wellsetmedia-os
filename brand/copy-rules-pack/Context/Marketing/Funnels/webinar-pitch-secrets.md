> Fourth file in the webinar canon. `mastering-webinars.md` is the operational SOP.
> `webinar-build-canon.md` covers belief targeting and break-even. `webinar-show-rate-playbook.md`

## 0. The one idea

Selling on a webinar is not a section at the end. It is the whole webinar, running quietly
underneath the teaching. By the time you say the price, the audience should already have
agreed with you seven or eight times, pictured themselves using the thing, and given you
explicit permission to sell. If the close feels like a gear change, you built it wrong.

The report's structure is deliberately backwards: it teaches the close first, then works
back to the content. Follow that order when you build. **Write the close before you write
the teaching.** The teaching exists to earn the close, so you cannot know what to teach
until you know what you are closing on.

---

## 1. Permission to close (say it in the first 60 seconds)

The single highest-leverage move in the whole report, and the one almost nobody does.
Within the first minute, before any content:

> "Now, at the end of the webinar I will be extending a special offer to you. However,
> I don't want you to invest in it. Let me say that again. I don't want you to invest in
> it, unless I make good on my promise to show you [restate the big promise].
>
> However, if I do show you how to [restate the promise again], then you should feel
> obligated to take advantage of my time-limited opportunity at the end of the call.
>
> Does that sound fair?"

Five things this buys you, all at once:

1. **Rapport.** They already know you are selling. Saying it first means you are not
   pretending, and they score you for it.
2. **A buying criterion.** The question stops being *will I buy* and becomes *I'll buy
   if*. You have handed them the condition, and it is a condition you control.
3. **No awkward transition later.** The offer is future-paced. When it arrives it is
   expected, by them and by you. Awkwardness at the transition kills more conversions
   than any other single thing.
4. **A tie-down.** "Does that sound fair?" gets the first yes and sets the tone for
   interactivity for the next 90 minutes.
5. **Attention.** Almost nobody opens this way, so they stay to see what else you do
   differently.

**Elevate note:** this is the opposite of what most of our decks do, which is hide the
offer until the transition slide. Run this on the next build and measure it. It pairs
cleanly with the 9-Agreements rule about not *revealing* the offer early. You are
announcing that an offer exists, not what it is.

## 2. Trial closes (run these through the teaching)

Trial closes warm people to the offer before it exists. They are not closes. Their job is
to make the audience build an identity, *I am someone who does this*, so that refusing
the offer later means contradicting themselves.

Three of them. Pepper all three through the content section.

### 2.1 "When are you going to do this?"

After any powerful point, ask it. Vary the phrasing so it stays fresh:

- If you haven't done this already, when's a good time to start?
- Do you think it'd be a good idea to do this the first chance you got?
- Now that you know this, when are you going to start using it?

Makes the watching active instead of passive, and puts them in the picture using it.

### 2.2 The mind picture

Formula: *"You can see how if you did [technique] ALONE, that could [benefit], couldn't it?"*
Then follow with "so when are you going to start doing this?"

Variations:

- That [technique] ALONE would help [benefit], wouldn't it?
- You'd agree that simply doing [technique] ALONE could [benefit], correct?
- It's fair to say that just doing [technique] by itself is enough to [benefit], right?

The point is the acknowledgement. Showing something powerful and moving on wastes it.

### 2.3 The sudden stop

Use once, roughly a third of the way into content:

> "If we stopped the webinar right here, just based on what I've shown you, you've
> probably already gotten more value than you get from most paid courses, yes?
> But we're just getting started. Wait until you see what I'm about to show you now."

Works on three levels: it reframes what they just got as more valuable than paid product;
it plays to the "if I could only do 10% of what he does I'd be happy" mentality, which
lowers the internal-belief bar; and it pre-frames the next block so they lean in.

## 3. Dramatic demonstration (one per webinar, non-negotiable)

The rule: **every webinar must contain at least one dramatic demonstration.** Not a claim,
not a testimonial, not a screenshot. A thing done live, in front of them, that proves the
claim beyond any words available to you.

Fladlien's examples: writing an article on a topic the audience picks, live, in seven
minutes. Writing and sending an email to his list at the start of the call, then showing
the revenue it produced before the call ended. A guest writing a book live, audience
picking the title, timed start to finish.

Outside marketing, the same move: Timex strapped to the bottom of a speedboat, still
ticking. A Zippo cut out of a fish's stomach, still lighting. Billy Mays refused to pitch
anything that could not be dramatically demonstrated.

**How to find yours.** Write the presentation first, without thinking about this at all.
Then walk back through it slide by slide asking one question: *is there a way I can
dramatically demonstrate this?* Ideas fall out fast.

One good demonstration is claimed to double or triple conversions. Treat that number as
his, not ours, but treat the technique as mandatory.

## 4. Micro-stories

A micro-story is 1-3 minutes long and exists to hang a memorable picture on one point.
Not a founder story, not a case study. One point, one picture.

**Worked example, the wood-panel station wagon.** Fladlien's dad drove him to school in a
1979 Pontiac station wagon with wood panelling on the sides. He was too shy to get his own
licence until he was 22. He shows a photo of the car. Then: if I wrote you the greatest
sales letter ever written for this car, and the price was $300,000, would you buy it? No.
And if I wrote you the worst sales letter in the world for it and the price was $3, you'd
snap it up.

That story does four jobs in two minutes: they get to know him, it proves an awkward shy
kid can learn this, it makes "the offer matters more than the writing" permanently
memorable, and it kills the "I can't write" objection dead.

**Craft rules:**

- Minor concrete details do the work. The wood panelling. Dad being a teacher at the same
  school. The photo. Specificity is what makes it land as true rather than as a parable.
- Build them the same way as demonstrations: write the presentation first, then walk back
  and ask where a micro-story would land a point better than the dry version does.
- They accumulate. Do this for a year and you have a grab bag you can pull from for any
  point you ever need to make.

## 5. The five pitch transitions

The failure mode is physical, not logical: the presenter gets nervous going into the
pitch, and the tonality and rhythm change. The audience hears it. **If you are not
comfortable making the offer, they will not be comfortable taking it.**

If you used the permission opener in §1 you already have cover. If you didn't, pick one:

| Transition | The line |
|---|---|
| **Memory Lane** | "Did you enjoy the time we spent together today? *(pause)* How'd you like to make this more than a one-time occasion? Here's how we can spend more time together..." |
| **I wouldn't be doing my job** | "If you got value from what I showed you for free, and that's the tip of the iceberg, then I wouldn't be doing my job if I didn't give you the chance to take it further." |
| **Cheesy** | "How many of you think I'm about to make a special offer? *(pause)* Well, I wouldn't want to disappoint you." |
| **Obvious Adam** | "What I just showed you is powerful. If you'd like, I can show you how to make it easier and more effective, for a fee. Are you interested?" |
| **Two Options** | "Preparing this I had two options. One, show you cool stuff for an hour and part ways. Two, extend an offer to those who want to put it into action the easiest way. I chose two, and I think you'll be glad I did." |

Memory Lane and Two Options are the ones that fit Elevate and client decks. Cheesy is
named honestly and should stay out of anything for an analytical audience.

## 6. The nine killer closes

These are stackable. You do not pick one. You run several in sequence, and each one gives
you a fresh, non-repetitive reason to ask for the order again.

### Close 1: "You'll forget everything"

The pre-frame close. Runs **before** you explain the offer, right after the transition.

> "There are problems with everything we did here today. I want to talk about them in the
> last few minutes to those of you who said *maybe*.
>
> Maybe there's something here. Maybe there's a way to [benefit]. Maybe you can [benefit].
> Maybe, maybe, maybe. If you said maybe, you have to be concerned with these problems.
>
> Bridging the gap between new information and implementation. Huge gap. Some of you think
> you got it. Psychologists tell us you don't. 48 hours from now you'll have forgotten
> everything I talked about, and half of what you *thought* as a result. 16 days from now
> you won't remember having been on this webinar.
>
> This works like Momma said. In one ear and out the other.
>
> Some of you took notes. I'm a note-taker too, so let's be straight. Somewhere you've got
> a place. Under the bed, in the attic, a desk drawer, the boot of a car you don't drive
> any more. That's where all the notes you've ever taken went, and that's where these are
> going too. So who's fooling who?
>
> You're not bridging that gap with one webinar. You need more than that. You need..."

Then into the offer. Best close in the report for anything with coaching, community,
accountability, tools or ongoing support attached, which is most of what we sell.

### Close 2: "I'll sleep well tonight, will you?"

The takeaway. Goes **last**, after everything else, to move the fence-sitters.

> "Whether or not you say yes today, I'll still sleep well tonight. My life will still be
> [what you have because you already solved this]. So I'll sleep well. But will you? Will
> you sleep knowing you could have done something to change what you're currently getting?
> Especially when you act right now and get [fast-action bonuses]."

It positions the offer as something they need more than you need to sell. Fladlien's live
version at a seminar used a real screenshot of $10,642 in sales from the previous day,
made while he was at the bar. Five more people ran to the back of the room.

**Elevate note:** this one is high-risk in our niches. It only works if the proof behind
"my life is fine either way" is real, specific and clearable. Without that it reads as
posturing. See the proof rules in each workspace's copy foundation.

### Close 3: The Columbo

Deliberately hold back one attractive piece of the offer or a bonus. After you have gone
through the whole offer, "remember" it:

> "Oh, and I forgot to mention. We also include [held-back item] for anyone who buys in
> the next 48 hours. Those of you who already bought should have an email waiting.
> So you can see plenty of people already bought thinking they were getting [less than
> that], at this price. That's how good this is."

Then restate the offer and ask again. Three effects: fence-sitters get a new reason; you
get permission to ask for the order again without it reading as pressure; and the deal
feels better because you undersold it first.

Discovered by accident, when a spontaneous half-price link mid-call converted half the
room. The spontaneity is what makes it feel like a better deal, so the delivery has to be
casual.

### Close 4: The Schoolboy

> "Imagine it's your first day of high school. Bright-eyed freshman, first class, ready to
> learn. The teacher walks in, drops a thick textbook on your desk and says 'there you go,
> I'll be back at the end of the semester for the final.' Same thing next class. And the
> one after that.
>
> If that's how school worked, how well would you have done? My guess is most of you would
> still be trying to pass ninth grade.
>
> That's exactly what happens when you buy an information product."

For selling anything above information: services, coaching, ongoing training, done-with-you.
It reframes cost as investment and explains why they failed before without blaming them.

### Close 5: Webinar-price-only

The offer is good until the webinar ends. Then the price rises or a bonus goes away.
Introduce it when you first go into the offer, remind through, and then actually do it on
camera: "OK, the webinar's done, I'm raising the price now", and change it in front of
them.

Those who were buying anyway feel rewarded. Those who weren't get a decision forced. Those
who think you're bluffing learn otherwise, and next time they believe you.

**The scarcity rule this belongs to:** every webinar must make something scarce, and
stacking beats single. Triple stack: a discount that dies when the call ends, a bonus that
dies in 24 hours, and an extra bonus for the first 10 buyers. That last one starts a race
to the order form.

### Close 6: The Q&A close

Not a close so much as a mode. After your first close or two, drop the formal closing and
start answering questions. Fladlien reports selling three hours and fifty-eight minutes
into a webinar. The rule he settled on: **if they're still asking and still buying, keep
going until you sell every last one or your voice gives out.**

**Answer pattern.** Read the question aloud → "that's a great question, I'm glad you asked"
(defuses tension, buys you seconds, sets a positive tone) → **answer the question with a
question that reframes it as a benefit** → answer → ask if there's anything else they want
to know about what they get when they sign up today.

Worked example, on access length:

> "Excellent question. If I understand you right, you want to be able to go back and
> reference the material whenever you need it, is that correct? If so, you'll be happy to
> know you have lifetime access when you sign up today. If you want to log in 739 days
> from now, you can. Is there anything else you want to know about what's inside?"

Two special cases where you break the pattern:

**The Mutineer.** Someone types "this is one big pitch fest". Read it out, by name, and
release them:

> "Richard just typed this in, [read it]. Richard, I'm sorry you feel that way, and
> nothing is keeping you or anyone who feels that way from leaving this call right now.
> I love what I do and I believe in what I have. And folks, that's a good example of an
> attitude that gets in the way, so if you get nothing else from today, get this:
> complaining and making money are mostly mutually exclusive. Moving on."

Alienating one person pulls everyone else closer, and it demonstrates you handling
pressure, which people want to be around. Deliver it cheerful, not defensive. Doing this
angry is a different move with the opposite result.

**The Flawed Millionaire.** Admit at least three real flaws in the product, ideally during
Q&A. "Does it work on Mac?" → "It doesn't, and we won't have a Mac build for a while. One
of the reasons we can price this where we do is that we didn't build it. Here's your
workaround, and here's everything it does do on PC." No product is perfect, and people
believe your claims more once you've conceded something. Be suspicious of anyone with
nothing bad to say about their own product.

### Close 7: "Get off this call now"

> "We're about to finish. If you invested in the offer, please stay on. Everyone else,
> please sign off now, I'll give you a minute. Thank you for coming, but I want to talk
> to the people who signed up."

Wait 30-60 seconds. Almost nobody leaves. That's the point. Then walk the "buyers" through
support, onboarding, how you treat customers, and an unadvertised bonus.

Fence-sitters get to sample being your customer before they are one. Buyers get their
decision reinforced, which cuts refunds. Double duty, costs three minutes.

### Close 8: The Buddy close

Get a successful customer on the call. At the close: "We've got John here who's been
through this. John, can I unmute you to share your experience?" Then get out of the way.

A live third-party testimonial beats a testimonial slide by a distance. Two is better than
one. This is the **one** exception to the never-unmute rule in §7.

### Close 9: "Why'd you buy?"

Needs a second monitor with the cart open. As orders land: "I see Sarah just came in.
Sarah, why'd you decide to say yes today?" While she types, do four or five more. Read the
answers out.

Now the reasons to buy are coming from buyers, not from you. Perfect social proof. If one
answer is especially good, ask to unmute them and you've stacked the Buddy close on top.

## 7. Explaining the offer

Seven elements. All seven appear, in roughly this order:

1. Proof elements
2. What they get
3. Why they should act immediately
4. The price and why it's a bargain
5. The risk they carry
6. The benefits they enjoy
7. How to get it

### 7.1 Proof and benefits are not close-section items

Both belong in the **whole** presentation. Teach a technique, then immediately: "here's one
result I got using this." Proof laced through content does double duty. It proves the
claim and it establishes why they should listen to you, which is why you can skip the
biography (§9.6).

You cannot have too much proof. The test: if you were on trial for a murder you didn't
commit, would you want your lawyer to present a *reasonable* amount of evidence?

Proof types: dramatic demonstrations, facts and statistics, testimonials and case studies,
personal results, and good information itself. Live polls generate your own statistics
on the fly.

**How to add it:** write the presentation without thinking about proof. Then walk back
through every claim and ask *can I validate this?*

### 7.2 The benefit brain dump

Ten-minute timer, before you write anything. Write every benefit the audience gets from
the content and from the offer.

Openers to unstick yourself: *You can… / You will be able to… / You no longer have to… /
Say goodbye to… / Enjoy… / Be content in the fact that…*

**Take each benefit two levels deep.** Example:

- "You can write emails in half the time it used to take."
- → "Now that you can, you could double your follow-up volume, which makes more money for
  the same effort."
- → "Now that you make more with the same effort, you can reinvest and reach the financial
  goal you're actually after."

Three distinct benefits from one.

Then, and this is the part people skip, **put the list away and write the presentation
without looking at it.** The benefits come out naturally and better placed than if you had
been consciously inserting them. Only go back to the list if a pass afterwards shows a
slide that's thin.

### 7.3 What they get, in modules

Break the offer into modules. Even an ebook: each chapter is a module. Each module gets a
headline that sells, then 4-6 benefit bullets.

His copy class, six weeks, six modules:

- Week 1: Creating Crushing Offers In 6 Minutes Or Less
- Week 2: How to Use Compelling Proof In Your Letters, Even If You Personally Have NONE
- Week 3: Communicating Value Without Benefits Or Features
- Week 4: Your Story & Scarcity In 60 Seconds
- Week 5: Developing Your Hook & Assembling Your Sales Letter Effortlessly
- Week 6: How To Perfectly Design And Format Your Sales Letter In Under 5 Minutes

Hold one bonus or one module back for the Columbo.

### 7.4 Bargain maniac: the three-step price frame

Always in this order:

1. **Dollars at a discount.** What they make or save dwarfs what they pay.
2. **Time saved, converted to money.** 50 hours saved, even at $5/hour, is $250 of value.
3. **The intangibles.** Time with family, peace of mind, safety, freedom, a life without
   the stress. Ask what that's worth. The answer is priceless.

The order matters. Starting on intangibles reads as fluff. Ending on them is what makes
the number feel small.

### 7.5 The guarantee

The version from the deck:

> "Get this and go through it immediately. If after 10 minutes you don't feel you've
> already got your money's worth, I demand you contact me and I'll return every penny,
> quietly and promptly, no questions asked. You only stand to gain by acting immediately."

"I demand" is the operative word, and the 10-minute window is what makes it feel real
rather than boilerplate.

### 7.6 The stack order

A working default, not a law:

1. Pitch transition
2. "You'll forget everything" to pre-frame
3. Explain the offer in modules, with benefits and proof
4. Price and why it's a bargain
5. Guarantee
6. Bonuses
7. Scarcity
8. **Ask for the order**
9. Webinar-price-only close
10. Buddy close
11. Why'd-you-buy close
12. Columbo close
13. Q&A close
14. "I'll sleep well tonight" close

## 8. Mindset and tonality

### 8.1 The fire alarm salesman

The mindset argument, and it is the one that makes the mechanics work. You sell fire
alarms. You walk into a house that is visibly broke. Most salespeople **throw the close**.
They pull their punches, don't really try, leave without the order, and congratulate themselves
for being decent.

A week later the house burns down.

Your first job is to build an offer worth far more than its price. Your second is to
communicate that value as hard as you can. The decision is theirs. Throwing the close is
not kindness, it is you protecting your own comfort at their expense.

If you genuinely hold this, you will not get nervous at the transition, your tonality
won't wobble, and the audience will feel comfortable buying because you feel comfortable
selling.

### 8.2 Tonality beats audio quality beats video

On a webinar, audio matters more than video, and **tonality matters more than microphone**.
He uses a $40 USB headset.

How to build it, in three steps:

1. Recall a time you were genuinely passionate about something. Really get into it, see
   it, hear it. You know it worked when you feel different.
2. Start talking about anything and listen to how your voice has changed. Then clear your
   head, go back to normal, speak again. Hear the difference. That awareness is the skill.
3. Five minutes before every webinar, deliberately get yourself into that state. Do it
   before every one. Within a few webinars the state gets conditioned to the trigger and
   arrives on its own.

Repeat the exercise with sincerity, remember a time you genuinely wanted to help someone,
because passion without sincerity sounds like a pitch.

**Elevate note:** this is the mechanism behind the read-aloud rule already in the vault.
A script that reads stiff out loud produces flat tonality no matter who delivers it. See
`feedback_ugc_read_aloud_test` and `feedback_ellis_spoken_script_voice`.

## 9. The content section, and the mistakes

### 9.1 Record Label Theory

When a label wants to sell a million albums, they don't put the worst track on the radio.
They give away the best one, free, all day.

**Give your single best secret away on the webinar.** No holding back. You cannot cover
everything in 45-60 minutes anyway, so what they buy is: the other secrets, the depth and
nuance on the one you gave, easy reference to all of it, and the bonuses.

Err on the side of too much. If they don't buy today, they'll show up to your next one,
which is worth more than a squeezed sale. And if you genuinely gave away too much, sell
the recording.

### 9.2 The "one of N" frame

How to give a lot without giving everything, without ever withholding:

> "There are only 5 types of headline I write. Here's the one that works best and that you
> can use in under 5 minutes almost every time."

> "We use 7 different ways to make a price feel like a bargain, and we usually stack them.
> If you just use this one it should lift your conversions. Here it is."

They get something complete and usable. They leave wondering about the other four, and the
other six, and what stacking would do. Nothing was withheld and everything was implied.
The thought you want in the room is: *if this is what he gives away free, what's in the
paid thing?*

### 9.3 Small and immediate beats big and delayed

Given two techniques, one that produces a small result within minutes of the call ending,
one that is far more powerful but pays off in weeks, **teach the small immediate one.**

It's what the audience would rather have, and it converts better, because they can picture
using it tonight. Then make the big delayed one part of the offer, and explain how the two
combine. Now they want the package to complete the thing you already started them on.

### 9.4 Structure the whole thing around instant gratification

The strongest opening in the report, and it dictates the content:

> "On this webinar I'm going to show you, in 60 minutes, how to [result]. Literally 15
> minutes after this call is over, before you go to bed tonight, you can put this into
> action. Follow it step by step and don't be surprised if by Thursday you have [result].
> Is that something you'd like to do?"

Promising an immediate result forces you to teach step by step, which makes the content
easy to lay out. You are teaching the corner-cut version: manual where the paid version is
automated, one traffic source where the paid version has all of them, the free tool where
the paid one scales.

**How to pick the topic:** look at the complete offer and ask *of everything in here, what
is one small A-to-Z thing I can show that, if followed, gives a quick result?*

### 9.5 The manual-then-automatic formula

The simplest webinar to build, and it works for affiliate offers, software, services, or
anything where the thing you sell replaces effort:

1. Teach the result being achieved **manually**, properly, so it genuinely works.
2. Let them feel how long it takes and where the human error creeps in.
3. Show the same thing done with the paid tool or the done-for-you version, in a fraction
   of the time and with better output.
4. Add a bonus available only for the next 24-48 hours.

People who can't afford it leave with a working method and are grateful. People who can
afford it just watched an unarguable demonstration of the gap.

### 9.6 The six mistakes

1. **Waiting until you're ready.** You won't be ready for the first one, or the fourth.
   Roughly 30 hours of live webinar time before it stops feeling awful. Instruction plus
   experience; nobody can hand you the second one.
2. **Treating a small room as a waste.** Same energy for 10 as for 1,000. One of his
   $30,000 clients first found him on a small call. And you have to run the call either
   way.
3. **Unmuting for questions.** Someone's mic won't work, someone has background noise,
   someone talks for 15 minutes about nothing, and everyone else tunes out and leaves.
   Type them in the chat, read them aloud. The single exception is the Buddy close.
4. **No scarcity.** Every webinar makes something scarce. Stack them (§6, close 5).
5. **Thinking only short term.** The "tell them WHAT, never HOW" advice is wrong. Giving
   real HOW content sells better now *and* later: people who can't afford the $500 thing
   go looking for something of yours they can afford. You will be judged in future for the
   webinar you run today.
6. **The biography.** Ten minutes of "why you should listen to me" is the most common
   mistake, including among experienced presenters. Show it instead: teach a technique,
   then give five specific results you got from it. Credibility and content in the same
   breath. If you insist on a bio slide, three minutes maximum.

## 10. What's dead, and what survives from the minor pieces

**Dead.** GoToWebinar-specific mechanics (poll UI, question-box export). The dual-monitor
chapter, which is now just "obviously". The type-friendly-URL chapter, which mattered only
because GoToWebinar had no clickable links; every current platform does, though buying the
spelled-out variant of a numeric domain is still free insurance.

**"Getting People On A Webinar" (3pp) is almost entirely dead.** It is 2010 traffic advice:
Warrior Forum WSOs, Twitter blasting, iContact single opt-in. Two things survive:

- **The paid-registration ladder.** Charge $1 for the first 10 seats, $2 for the next 10,
  $3 for the next 10. His claim: free registrations show at around 5%, paid ones show far
  higher and buy far more, even at a dollar. Worth a test against our current cold-traffic
  show rates. Note this conflicts with the free-webinar model in `webinar-show-rate-playbook.md`;
  that file still wins for cold paid traffic until we have our own data.
- **The JV webinar prerequisite.** Before approaching a partner, record the webinar even
  with no attendees, and write the promo emails for them. Partners with lists who already
  run JV webinars need no education, only evidence. Approaching without a recording and
  ready-made emails is what makes these conversations die.

**The Info Product Avalanche Effect (21pp) is not training.** It is a registration lead
magnet, and a good specimen of one. What it does structurally, in order: origin story with
one absurdly small concrete number ($4 ebook, 7 pages, typo in the first sentence), three
named student results with times attached, market-size proof from named third parties
(Gartner, Straits Research), the one big mistake everyone makes stated but *not* solved,
an explicit "why am I sharing this" section, a mechanism named and half-explained, and
four separate registration CTAs across 20 pages. Every open loop resolves on the webinar
and nowhere else. Use it as the template when we build a PDF whose only job is to drive
registrations.

## 11. The build order

1. Decide the offer. Nothing else can start.
2. Benefit brain dump, 10 minutes, then put the list away.
3. Write the close: transition, offer in modules, price frame, guarantee, scarcity, then
   the stack of named closes.
4. Pick the one small A-to-Z thing you'll teach that produces an immediate result.
5. Write the content section as step-by-step teaching of that thing.
6. Walk back through and add proof to every claim.
7. Walk back through and add dramatic demonstrations.
8. Walk back through and add micro-stories where a point is dry.
9. Walk back through and add trial closes.
10. Write the opening: big promise, permission to close, tie-down.
11. Read the whole thing out loud. Anything stiff gets rewritten.

Steps 6-9 are four separate passes, each asking one question. Do not try to do them at
once, and do not try to do them while writing.
