# Offer Structuring Module

> Activates when `offer-structuring` is listed in knowledge.md `modules_active`.

## What This Module Adds

Deep copy-specific offer presentation methodology beyond the core SKILL.md protocol. SKILL.md covers the universal copywriting frameworks (awareness levels, market sophistication, headlines, proof pyramid, objection handling, CTAs). This module adds the format-specific methodology for presenting offers through written copy: outcome stack copywriting (we do NOT dollar-value-stack — see below), guarantee framing language patterns, price anchoring copy, payment plan presentation, bonus copy structure, urgency and scarcity copy that maintains trust, ROI math presentation, comparison table copy, and "what you get" section architecture. Built for copywriters who need to take a structured offer and present it in a way that maximizes perceived value and minimizes purchase friction across any deliverable format (sales page, VSL, email, ad).

## Outcome Stack Copywriting

> **Positioning rule (AID): we do NOT dollar-value-stack.** Assigning a $ value to each component and summing a "Total Value: $24,700 — but today only $2,497" is outdated, transparently inflated, and erodes trust. Position every component by the **outcome and transformation it delivers**, and anchor the price against the **cost of the unsolved problem** — never against a fabricated component total. (Per the standing AID no-value-stacking rule.)

The offer section is the most important part of any sales argument. Each component must be presented individually with its own mini-pitch before being assembled into the full stack. Listing features without framing them is the same as handing someone a menu with no descriptions.

### How to Present Each Component

Every component follows this 4-element structure (outcome-led, no assigned dollar value):

**Element 1: Component Name**
Give every component a specific, benefit-oriented name. "Module 3" is not a name. "The Client Acquisition Engine" is a name. Named things feel more valuable than unnamed things.

**Naming patterns:**
- The [Outcome] [System/Framework/Blueprint]: "The Revenue Acceleration Framework"
- [Number]-[Unit] [Outcome] [Noun]: "90-Day Client Pipeline Blueprint"
- The [Adjective] [Noun]: "The Complete Playbook" / "The Fast-Track Toolkit"

**Element 2: What It Does (Not What It Is)**
Describe the component in terms of the outcome it produces, not its format or contents.

Wrong: "A 47-page PDF guide on lead generation strategies."
Right: "The exact lead generation system that fills your calendar with 15-20 qualified prospects every week without cold calling or paid ads."

The reader does not care about the format. They care about the result.

**Element 3: What It Replaces**
Show the reader what they would need without this component. This creates the value comparison.

"Without this, you'd need to hire a $5,000/month marketing agency to build and manage your lead generation system. Or spend 6 months testing strategies yourself, burning through $10K+ in ad spend to figure out what works."

**Element 4: The Transition to the Next Component**
After each component, bridge to the next one. "And to make sure [Component 1] produces results even faster, I'm also including..."

### Outcome Stack Order

Present components in this sequence for maximum perceived momentum:

| Order | Component Type | Purpose |
|-------|---------------|---------|
| 1 | Core offer | The main thing. This is what they're buying. Lead with it. |
| 2 | Implementation accelerator | Makes the core offer work faster. "So you get results in weeks, not months." |
| 3 | Obstacle remover | Eliminates the biggest barrier to success. "The thing that stops most people from getting results with [core offer]." |
| 4 | Protection component | Prevents common failures. Templates, checklists, review systems. "So you never [common mistake]." |
| 5 | Bonus 1 (problem-adjacent) | Solves a related problem they haven't asked about. "And because [related problem] often comes up, I'm including..." |
| 6 | Bonus 2 (convenience/speed) | Saves time or adds ease. Done-for-you templates, swipe files, plug-and-play resources. |
| 7 | Bonus 3 (surprise/delight) | Optional. Something unexpected that adds novelty. Community access, live calls, exclusive resource. |

### Outcome Stack Copy Template

Use this template for each component:

```
[COMPONENT NAME]
[1-2 sentence outcome description — the transformation it delivers]

[1-2 sentences: the painful, slow, or expensive alternative without it]
```

After all components, transition straight to the price — anchored on the cost of inaction or ROI (see Price Anchoring), then a single clean price. No "total value" line, no "but you're not paying $X":

```
Everything above, working together to [core transformation].

Your investment: $[actual price]
[Or X payments of $amount]
```

## Guarantee Framing Language Patterns

The guarantee is a copywriting tool, not a legal clause. How you frame it determines whether it removes fear or gets ignored.

### Pattern 1: The Named Guarantee

Give the guarantee a proper name. Named guarantees feel more substantial than generic ones.

**Structure:**
"The [Name] Guarantee: [Clear terms in one sentence]. [What happens if they're not satisfied]. [Risk reversal statement]."

**Example:**
"The 90-Day Revenue Guarantee: Implement the system, follow the process, and if you don't add a minimum of $10,000 in new revenue within 90 days, email us at [address] for a complete refund. You're not risking $2,497. You're risking another 90 days of the same results you've been getting."

### Pattern 2: The Try-Before-You-Decide Frame

Position the guarantee as a test drive, not a return policy.

**Structure:**
"Try [Offer Name] for [timeframe]. Put it to work in your business. [Specific action they should take]. If after [timeframe] you don't [specific measurable result], [refund process]. You're essentially getting to test this with zero risk."

### Pattern 3: The Better-Than-Money-Back Frame

Goes beyond refund to make the guarantee itself feel valuable.

**Structure:**
"If [Offer Name] doesn't [specific result] within [timeframe], I'll refund every dollar AND [additional compensation]. You'll walk away with [what they keep] plus [additional value]. That's how confident I am that this works."

### Pattern 4: The Conditional Performance Frame

For high-ticket offers where an unconditional guarantee could attract freebie-seekers.

**Structure:**
"Here's the deal: follow the system. Complete [specific milestones]. Submit your [deliverables/assignments/implementation proof]. If after doing the work, you don't see [result], I'll refund your investment in full. This guarantee protects people who implement. It's designed for action-takers."

### Guarantee Language Rules

- State the guarantee in second person. "You get..." not "The purchaser shall receive..."
- Include the exact refund process. "Email [address]" or "Fill out the form at [URL]." Vague refund processes increase anxiety.
- Quantify the guarantee period. "90 days" is concrete. "Satisfaction guaranteed" is meaningless.
- Repeat the guarantee in miniature near every CTA below the main guarantee section. "Protected by our 90-Day Revenue Guarantee."
- Never bury the guarantee. It should have visual prominence: distinct section, bold heading, badge or seal.

## Price Anchoring Copy

Anchoring is the technique of establishing a reference point that makes the actual price feel reasonable by comparison. Every price needs context.

### Anchoring Methods

> Anchor against **real external costs** — the alternative, the problem, the timeline — never a summed "total value of components." Fabricated component totals don't survive scrutiny; real costs do.

**Method 1: Cost of inaction (lead with this)**
Calculate what the problem costs them over time. The strongest, most honest anchor.

"If your current close rate is 15% and it should be 30%, you're losing roughly half your potential revenue every month. On a $10,000/month business, that's $5,000/month in lost revenue. Over the next 12 months, that's $60,000 sitting on the table. The investment to fix it: $997."

**Method 2: Alternative cost comparison**
Compare the price to what the reader is currently spending (or would spend) to achieve the same result without the offer.

"Most business owners spend $3,000-5,000/month on a marketing agency. That's $36,000-60,000 per year. [Offer Name] gives you the same system for a one-time investment of $2,497. You'd break even in less than one month of saved agency fees."

**Method 3: Daily cost breakdown**
Divide the price by the number of days of access or expected benefit.

"$2,497 divided by 365 days is $6.84 per day. Less than your morning coffee for a system that generates $10K+ months."

**Method 4: ROI multiplier**
Show the return on investment as a multiple.

"The average client sees a 5-7x return within the first 90 days. That means a $2,497 investment generates $12,000-17,000 in new revenue. You're not spending $2,497. You're trading $2,497 for $12,000."

### Anchoring Rules

- Use 2-3 anchoring methods per offer. One anchor is not enough. Layer them.
- Always present anchors BEFORE the price. Anchors placed after the price feel like justification. Anchors before the price feel like context.
- Use specific numbers in anchors, not round ones. "$4,850/month in lost revenue" is more believable than "about $5,000."
- If the anchor is a comparison to competitors or alternatives, the numbers must be verifiable. Inflated anchors damage trust.

## Payment Plan Presentation Copy

Payment plans exist to remove the cash-flow objection. Present them as options, not downgrades.

**Presentation structure:**

```
OPTION 1 (Best Value): One payment of $[full price]
  - Save $[difference between payment plan total and full price]
  - Immediate full access

OPTION 2 (Most Flexible): [X] payments of $[amount]
  - Same complete access from day one
  - Spread your investment over [duration]
```

**Payment plan copy rules:**
- Always present the full payment first. It becomes the anchor for the payment plan.
- Frame the payment plan as flexibility, not inability to afford. "Spread your investment" not "Can't afford the full price?"
- Both options get the same access. Never gate components behind the full-payment option. That punishes payment plan buyers and increases refund requests.
- If offering more than two payment options, label one as "Most Popular." Social proof on the option reduces decision paralysis.
- State what happens after the plan is complete. "After [X] payments, you own lifetime access." Clarity prevents objections.

## Bonus Copy: Making Bonuses Feel Valuable

Bonuses that feel like afterthoughts reduce perceived value. Bonuses that feel like standalone products increase it.

### Bonus Presentation Structure

Each bonus follows this sequence:

1. **Transition from the core offer:** "And because [reason related to the core offer], I'm also including..."
2. **Bonus name:** Specific, benefit-oriented name. "The Quick-Start Revenue Templates" not "Bonus Templates."
3. **The problem it solves:** Connect the bonus to a real concern. "Most people get stuck on [specific step]. This eliminates that entirely."
4. **What's inside (briefly):** 1-2 sentences. Not a detailed breakdown. Enough to make it tangible.
5. **Why it matters:** "On its own, this unlocks [tangible outcome]." Frame by the outcome it delivers — do NOT assign it a separate dollar price.
6. **Why it's included:** "Because [reason], it's included at no extra charge when you join [Offer Name] today."

### Bonus Copy Rules

- Maximum 3 bonuses. More than 3 dilutes the core offer and makes the value stack feel padded.
- Each bonus must solve a DIFFERENT problem than the core offer. Bonuses that overlap with the main product feel like the main product is incomplete.
- At least one bonus should be a "speed" bonus: something that helps them get results faster. Speed bonuses are the highest-perceived-value add-on.
- Never let a bonus overshadow the core offer. The core offer is always the star; bonuses are supporting actors that each remove a specific obstacle.
- Frame bonus removal as urgency (only if real): "This bonus is available for the first 100 members" or "I'm removing [Bonus Name] after [date]."

## Urgency and Scarcity Copy That Maintains Trust

Urgency and scarcity are the most abused tools in marketing copy. Fake urgency trains audiences to ignore you. Real urgency with honest framing drives action without eroding trust.

### Legitimate Urgency Triggers

| Trigger | Copy Pattern | Why It's Legitimate |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Cohort-based enrollment | "Enrollment closes [date]. The next cohort starts [date]." | The program has a real start date and cannot accept students mid-cohort. |
| Limited capacity | "[X] spots remaining. We cap enrollment to maintain [quality/support/access]." | There is a real operational reason for the limit. |
| Price increase | "Price goes to $[new price] on [date]. Lock in the current rate before then." | The price is genuinely changing (and stays at the new level afterward). |
| Bonus expiration | "[Bonus Name] is available for the first [X] people who enroll." | The bonus genuinely goes away. Do not bring it back the next day. |
| Seasonal relevance | "Start before Q4 to have the system running by Black Friday." | There is a real external deadline tied to the reader's business. |

### Illegitimate Urgency (Never Use)

| Tactic | Why It Fails |
|--------|-------------|
| Evergreen countdown timers that reset per visitor | The reader can open an incognito window and see the timer reset. Instantly destroys trust. |
| "Only 3 spots left" on a digital product with no capacity limit | Digital products don't run out. The audience knows this. |
| "Closing tonight" on an offer that reopens tomorrow | One bad experience and the reader ignores all future deadlines. |
| "Limited time offer" with no specified end date | Without a specific date, "limited time" means nothing. |

### Urgency Copy Structure

**Opening urgency statement:**
State the constraint clearly and specifically. "Enrollment for the Spring 2026 cohort closes on April 15th at midnight PT. After that, the next enrollment window opens in September."

**Reason why:**
Explain WHY the constraint exists. "We cap each cohort at 30 members because every participant gets a personalized implementation review from our team. With more than 30 members, the quality of that review drops. We won't let that happen."

**What they lose by waiting:**
Specific, concrete consequences. "If you wait until September, that's 5 months of [continued problem]. At your current [metric], that costs approximately $[amount]."

**Final CTA with urgency:**
"Join the Spring cohort before [date]. Lock in your spot and start the system this month."

## ROI Math Presentation

ROI math is the most persuasive price justification tool. It transforms the purchase from a cost into an investment with calculable returns.

### ROI Math Copy Structure

**Step 1: Establish the current baseline.**
"Right now, your [relevant metric] is [current number]."
Pull this from the audience's typical starting point. If you don't have exact data, use the common range: "Most [audience type] we work with start at [range]."

**Step 2: State the realistic improvement.**
"Our average client improves their [metric] by [percentage or amount] within [timeframe]."
Use conservative numbers. Understating the improvement is more credible than overstating it.

**Step 3: Calculate the dollar impact.**
"If you're currently at [baseline] and improve to [new number], that's an additional $[amount] per [time period]."
Show the math step by step. Don't just state the result. Let them follow the calculation.

**Step 4: Compare to the investment.**
"Your investment: $[price]. Your projected return in [timeframe]: $[amount]. That's a [X]x return."

**Step 5: Extend the timeline.**
"And that's just the first [timeframe]. Over the next 12 months, the same improvement compounds to $[amount]."

### ROI Math Rules

- Use conservative projections, not best-case scenarios. If your average client gets 5x ROI, present 3x in the copy and let the real results exceed expectations.
- Show your work. Don't just state "$50,000 in new revenue." Walk through the calculation: "[X] new clients x $[average deal size] x [close rate improvement] = $[result]."
- Acknowledge the variable: "Your results will depend on [key factors]. These numbers are based on our average client with [baseline characteristics]."
- If possible, use the reader's own numbers. In interactive contexts (sales calls, live presentations), ask for their metrics and run the calculation live.

## Comparison Table Copy

Comparison tables position your offer against alternatives. When done well, they make the decision obvious. When done poorly, they look defensive.

### Comparison Table Structure

**Column headers:**
| Feature/Benefit | Doing It Yourself | Hiring an Agency | [Offer Name] |
|---|---|---|---|

**Row categories to include:**

| Category | What to Compare |
|----------|----------------|
| Time to results | Weeks/months for each option |
| Total cost (12-month view) | One-time vs. recurring costs |
| Ongoing support | What happens after the initial engagement |
| Customization | How personalized the solution is |
| Learning curve | How hard it is to implement |
| Guarantee/risk | What happens if it doesn't work |
| Outcome specificity | How targeted the results are |

### Comparison Table Copy Rules

- Never name competitors directly. Compare against categories: "Typical Agency," "DIY Approach," "Freelancer." Naming competitors makes you look insecure and can create legal issues.
- The comparison should be fair. If the competitor genuinely does something better, don't hide it. Acknowledge it and show why your advantage matters more.
- Use checkmarks, X marks, and brief phrases. Do not write paragraphs inside table cells.
- Place the comparison table near the price section. It reframes the price as the smart choice relative to alternatives.
- Highlight your offer column visually (bold header, background color, "Recommended" badge).

## "What You Get" Section Structure

The "what you get" section is the tangible breakdown of the offer. It translates the value stack into a scannable, visually organized format.

### Section Structure

**Section headline:** Benefit-oriented. "Everything You Need to [Outcome]" not "What's Included."

**Component list:** Each item on its own line or card with:
- Icon or visual marker
- Component name (bolded)
- One-line description (what it does, not what it is)
- Value callout (right-aligned or below the description)

**Visual treatment:**
- Alternate background colors for each component (white/light gray) to improve scannability.
- Use product mockups or screenshots alongside text descriptions. Tangible visuals increase perceived value.
- Separate core components from bonuses with a clear divider. "PLUS, when you join today, you also get..."

**Section closer:** Outcome recap, then price transition — no total-value line, no "% off."

"Everything you need to [core outcome] — in one place, ready to use.
Your investment today: $[price]."

### "What You Get" Copy Rules

- Every component gets a description. A bare list of names with no context is a missed conversion opportunity.
- Descriptions focus on outcomes, not features. "The Lead Generation Engine: Fill your calendar with 15+ qualified calls per week" not "The Lead Generation Engine: 12 video modules on lead generation."
- If a component is a digital asset (PDF, template, course), include a visual mockup. A PDF icon next to a name looks generic. A designed mockup of the asset looks tangible and valuable.
- Order components by perceived value (highest first). The reader's attention is strongest at the top of the list.

- [ ] Every component has a specific, benefit-oriented name (not "Module 1" or "Bonus Template")

- [ ] Each component frames the cost/pain of the alternative without it (not a fabricated retail $ value)
